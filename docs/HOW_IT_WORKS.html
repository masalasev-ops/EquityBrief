How EquityBrief works

1. What it is, and what it will not do

What problem does this tool solve?

Every evening, after the US market closes, EquityBrief looks at every stock in the S&P 500. It picks the few that are at a buy point for a swing trade, a position held for a few weeks, under three setups, each a rule of its own: a pullback into support, a breakout to a new high, and the drift after an earnings beat. Those form tonight's list.

From October 2026 the night also reads every stock in the S&P 400 and the S&P 600, the mid-sized and smaller companies, about 1,500 stocks in all, each tagged with its index. Their prices, levels and reported quarters are stored and worked out every night like the S&P 500's. Each index has rules of its own, its stocks ranked and judged against its own index rather than against the largest companies, with a minimum price, a minimum daily dollar volume, a check that the company made money over its last four quarters, and a trading cost taken off every result. Those rules are provisional: they list picks from the first night, marked "Provisional: not yet frozen", and none is relied on until you freeze it after its test over the history.

Each night also stores, for every stock in all three indices, the readings those rules and their tests read: how many dollars trade in it on an average day, what a round trip in it costs at the published spreads for its size and price, whether the company made money and covers its interest, how near it sits to its year's high, how heavy its volume was, and how the largest companies in its industry have done over the last month and quarter. A stock's page shows them in a card of their own, beside how many analysts rate the company, which no rule reads yet. The S&P 500's rules keep their records as frozen, and the Run page now states beside each one's edge what that edge is once each trade's own round trip is taken off.

Every page has a selector labelled Universe at its top, offering the S&P 500, the S&P 400 and the S&P 600 with how many members each holds. A page reads one index at a time, and its heading and every count say which: "Tonight: S&P 400", "2 of 400 S&P 400 members passed a setup", "the S&P 400's breadth". Each card describes its rule in words the code writes from the rule's own settings, so a setting changed changes the words. A stock is traded once across every card of every index: one held on the S&P 400's list is not listed on the S&P 500's until that trade ends, and the card's note says which index holds it.

When you freeze a setup on the S&P 400 or 600, one command registers its rule and up to eight variants beside it, each at settings that index's test over the history read. From the night after, the frozen rule draws that setup's card in place of the provisional one, its card reading "Live rule since" the day you froze it, and each rule keeps its own list of trades, each trade's result counted after its own round trip and against the same plan placed on every stock of that index that night. The index's Run page then shows each rule's record, and a rule's record counts only from its freeze. The sector heavyweights freeze the same way in either of the two designs their test read, the index's own leaders or the followers of the S&P 500's leading industries: each rule keeps a book of holdings of its own, and the frozen rule's book is the one the card shows.

Each pick on every index also carries a card of its own, opened in place beneath its row on Tonight and drawn at the top of the stock's page. It holds five checks on the trade, each a tick, a note or a warning with its reason in words: the gates the pick passed, whether the company made money and covers its interest, how the index's breadth and the stock's sector stand, whether its next report falls inside the time by which three in four of the rule's trades had ended, and how much trades in it a day with what a round trip costs against the trade's risk. Beneath them stands the record of the rule that picked it, replayed over the stored history after each trade's cost, under the rule's own heading: it is the rule's record over every stock it bought, not this stock's chance. The card advises and removes no pick.

On Tonight a sixth check counts the trades you hold open in the stock's sector across the three indices, and warns at two, since stocks in one sector tend to move together. Once you enter your account's size, the risk you take a trade and the most one position may hold on the Account page, the card turns its plan into shares: as many as the risk buys over the distance to the stop, rounded down, and never more than the position cap, a fifth of the account unless you change it. Those three settings stay in a file on your machine and in nothing the tool stores, logs or exports. The card also says how the trade is managed in its rule's own terms, each stop a sale at the close of a day that closes below it. A Taken button records a trade you took, at the next day's open unless you enter your own price, and later its exit.

The card also lists what could hit the trade before it ends: how far the stock has moved on its past earnings reports against this plan's stop, the next ex-dividend date and what the dividend takes off the price, or, where the tool holds none of the company's dividends yet, that a later one cannot be ruled out, and any Federal Reserve decision or inflation report due inside the hold, days the whole market tends to move more. Each night the tool follows the trades you took under their rule's own selling rules, and shows your record of each rule beside the rule's own, counting how many you won and lost and giving your average result once twenty have ended. Beneath it the card counts the rule's own picks on the same nights you took one, each bought at the plan's price, so a gap between the two is your choice of picks and your fills rather than the market of those weeks. Past picks lists them under Your trades. None of this changes which stocks are picked.

Any stock in the index can also be opened on its own. It opens to a full research report: the chart, the prices that matter, a plan, the numbers, and a written view of the company.

It does not predict prices.It finds prices a stock has respected before and says what to do if it gets there again.
It does not rank companies as investments.It judges setups on the chart. What it says of a business, whether its sales and margins are growing, is read from the company's reported quarters by fixed rules.
It does not choose your position's size.It says where to buy and sell. A pick's card works out shares only from the account size and the risk a trade you enter yourself, and the written report sizes nothing.
It spends money only where you have said it may.The nightly arithmetic is free. Written research costs money, for six stocks the night lists, taken in turn from the S&P 500's, 400's and 600's pages, and for any you open, and so does sorting a listed stock's news, each kept inside limits you set.

Why these limits. A forecast or a ranking would need evidence the tool does not have. Levels, plans and their outcomes can each be checked, so the tool keeps to what can be checked.

2. One night, in one picture

What happens after the close, and what happens when you read?

How to read it. Blue is the nightly arithmetic, teal is what you see, purple is research, the paid step, which the night also asks for on the first six stocks its page lists.

What to take from it. Everything you look at was worked out the night before. The first six stocks the night lists have their research queued and written the next day at 16:30 UTC, outside the research model's dearer hours, with their news labelled first; every other stock's waits for you to open it.

Why split it this way. Five hundred stocks every night must be cheap and fast, so the night's own work is arithmetic. Written analysis is slow and costs money, so it is written for the few stocks the night lists first and for the ones you open, and it is kept. After its arithmetic the night starts that research and the labelling of the listed stocks' news as work of their own, each paid at the cheaper hours, waiting for them where the night ends inside the dearer ones, and stopped at its own limits.

A copy of the store every night. Once the night, the reports it asked for and the news labelling have all finished, the whole store is copied into a folder of its own. Each copy is opened and read back against the store before it counts, and the newest three are kept, so a damaged night still leaves the two before it. The Run page says when the newest copy was made and where it is. A copy guards against a damaged file; one kept on the same disk does not guard against the disk failing.

3. Words used in this guide

The few terms everything else depends on.

Session
One trading day.
Support
A price where the stock has stopped falling before.
Resistance
A price where the stock has stopped rising before.
Band
A support or resistance level drawn as a narrow price range, because prices a few cents apart cannot be told apart.
Swing high, swing low
A local peak or trough: a day higher, or lower, than the days around it.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 sessions.
Retracement
A price a set fraction of the way back between the last swing high and swing low.
Volume shelf
A price range where an unusually large share of recent trading happened.
Typical daily move
How far this stock moves on an ordinary day. Used as a ruler, so "two typical days away" means the same for a calm stock and a volatile one.
Trend label
Uptrend, range or downtrend, read from the moving averages and the swings.
Pullback
A dip back toward support after a rise.
Tranche
One step of a purchase made in steps.
Stop
The price at which a step is sold because it was wrong. Here it means a close below that price, not a touch.
Invalidation
The price below which the whole idea is wrong.
Reward to risk
The distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop.
Break-even rate
How often a plan must reach its target before its stop just to come out even.
Breadth
The share of the index trading above its own 200-day average. A reading of the whole market's health.
Relative strength
How a stock's recent return compares with every other stock in the index.
Setup
One kind of buy point with a rule of its own: the pullback, the breakout or the earnings drift. Each lists at most five stocks a night.
Variant
A copy of a setup's rule with one setting changed, scored in the background beside it and never listed.
Freeze
Fixing a setup's settings, on your word, from what its sweep of the history found. Only the trades after a freeze count toward its record.
Settings version
The set of thresholds the pullback's filter runs with each night. A change opens a new version; the old one and its record are kept.
Calibration
Adjusting a setting from what the system has measured, and only with your approval.
Learned score
An order for a rule's list fitted by code to the setups of the past: a weighted sum, and beside it a challenger of decision trees. It never lists a stock its rule's gates refuse.

4. Finding the prices that matter

Where has this stock repeatedly stopped falling or rising?

How to read it. The line is the price over a few months. Orange bands sit above the price, green bands below it. Each open circle is a day the price turned at a band.

What to take from it. A band that turned the price several times is stronger evidence than one that turned it once.

Four kinds of evidence become one band

How to read it. Each box on the left is one reason a price might matter. Where several reasons land close together, they become one band.

What to take from it. A band backed by several kinds of evidence, touched often and recently, scores highest.

Why levels at all. Support and resistance levels have been found to mark where prices pause more often than chance would give, and to sit where buy and sell orders cluster (Osler 2000; Chung and Bellotti 2021; Kavajecz and Odders-White 2004).

Why volume shelves. Where many shares changed hands, many holders have that price as their cost. They tend to defend it on the way down and sell near it on the way up.

5. Turning those prices into a plan

If I wanted to buy this stock, where would I buy, where would I be wrong, and where would I sell?

How to read it. One vertical price scale. The dark line is tonight's close. Green is where to buy, orange is where to sell, dashed lines are stops, red is where the idea fails.

What to take from it. Every number in a plan comes from a band on the chart, so every instruction can be traced to a price the stock has respected before.

The trend label decides the plan's shape

How to read it. An uptrend means the price is above its 50 and 200-day averages with each swing low higher than the last. A downtrend is the reverse. Anything else is a range.

What to take from it. The same bands give a different plan depending on the trend, and a falling stock is never given a buying plan.

Why buy in steps. A support band is a zone, not a single price, and nobody knows where in it the price will turn. Spreading the purchase across bands means being partly right is still useful (Brennan, Li and Torous 2005).

Why stops on a close. Prices dip through levels during the day far more often than they close through them. A stop on a touch is taken out by ordinary noise; a stop on a close is taken out by a real decision. Overnight gaps can still jump past a stop, which is why the report states the gap risk around earnings (Arratia and Dorador 2019).

Why no position sizes. How much to buy depends on your money and your tolerance for loss. The report shows the arithmetic from a risk budget you choose, and never picks one for you.

6. Choosing tonight's list

Out of five hundred stocks, which are at a buy point tonight?

Tonight's page is three swing setups, each a rule of its own with a card of its own: the pullback, the breakout and the earnings drift, and after them a fourth card, the sector heavyweights, a rotation held for months. The pullback's rule is the filter drawn below, five gates every stock must pass; the others follow it.

How to read it. Each layer is one test. A stock must pass every layer to reach the bottom.

What to take from it. Each card is short on purpose, and some nights every card is empty. The market gate closes every swing setup's card at once, and the line at the top of the page says whether the market left the lists open; the sector heavyweights' card is not closed by it.

GateWhat it asksWhy it is there
The marketIs a large enough share of the index above its long-term average?Buying dips works poorly when most stocks are falling together.
Trend and strengthIs this stock rising, and has it done better than most others lately?Stocks that have outperformed over recent months have tended to keep outperforming (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993; George and Hwang 2004).
A setupHas it pulled back into support from a recent high, on volume that dried up on the way down?Short-term dips tend to partly reverse (Jegadeesh 1990; Lehmann 1990), and a real level gives a clear place to be wrong. The filter once read breakouts from a tight base here too; they found about five stocks a year, so breakouts now have a setup of their own, below.
A fresh triggerDid the buy signal fire in the last few sessions?The list shows moments, not stocks that have been sitting at a price for weeks.
A trade worth takingFrom tonight's price, is the target far enough away compared with the stop?A good chart with a poor payoff is not a trade. Measuring from tonight's price also drops any stock that has already run away from its buy point.
ExclusionsIs an earnings report due within the holding period? Are the prices in doubt?An earnings report can jump straight past a stop (Dubinsky and others 2019).

The exact values each gate uses are in the specification. The pullback was frozen at them on 2026-10-02, with the minimum reward for the risk raised to 2, and calibration, described in section 10, moves them only on your word.

Improving businesses first. Among the stocks that pass, the pullback's card puts first those whose business is improving: sales above a year earlier and the operating margin wider than a year earlier, in both of the last two quarters. Steady businesses come next, then those with too few quarters or none stored yet, then those deteriorating, and inside each group the order stays reward to risk, then strength, then support. Every stock that passes is still listed. The order decides what comes first, not what is recommended, and no gate reads the business at all. A night drawn before the readings existed keeps the order it was drawn in.

The breakout. A stock closes above its highest price of the past six months on heavy volume, after its daily ranges narrowed. It is bought at the close with its stop one and a half typical moves below, raised as the price climbs and never lowered, and no target, so a winner is held while it keeps climbing. Its card lists the heaviest volume against its own average first.

The earnings drift. A company beats its estimate and the stock closes up at least half a typical day's move on twice its usual volume. It is bought within three sessions of that day while it holds above that day's low, with its stop at that low and its target at the first band at least two typical moves above the buy or two and a half times the risk, whichever is nearer. Its card lists the largest earnings surprise first.

The sector heavyweights, a fourth card held for months. On the first trading day of each month, or the first after it whose stored prices hold what its readings need, the night ranks each of the eleven sectors' companies by their size as it stood that day, counting a company once however many share classes it lists, and looks at the ten largest. The two whose return over the past twelve months beat their sector's fund by the most are bought at that close, where each beat the fund at all, its price sits above its 50-day average and that above its 200-day, and it has moved at least as much as the market over the past year, a beta of one or more. It has no stop and no target: it is held while it keeps leading, and sold at the close of a later month's rebalance where the rule would no longer buy it, or at its last close before it leaves the index. Its result is read in percent, beside what its sector's ten largest made over the same days, so its record asks whether leading picked well among the largest rather than whether the largest did well. The card shows each holding with its lead and its price against its 200-day average, what the last rebalance bought and sold, and the next rebalance's date. These are the settings its sweep over the years since 2019 proposed and the operator froze on 2026-10-04: over 374 trades its holdings beat their sector's ten largest by 1.34 points a trade, in 6 of the 8 years. Three variants run beside it, each with one setting moved: one also sells at a close under the 200-day average, one reads the sector's return as its stocks' average rather than its fund's, and one ranks every company of the sector. Each keeps a book and a record of its own, which the run page reads; the card draws the live rule's. A rebalance waits for a night the store holds every fund's close and the index's, so a series the night could not fetch moves no holding. It also waits for a night whose stored year of prices holds the 252 closes a twelve-month return and a beta read, the closes of 251 trading days and the one before them. The store keeps a calendar year, which on a Monday whose date a year back was a Sunday holds one close fewer, so a month that opens on a Monday is read on the Tuesday; the card names that day and says the rebalance waits. A rebalance that reads no lead, or no beta, for any company waits the same way, rather than selling everything on figures it never read. The night asks for a report on a stock it buys, after the swing setups' picks, and never on one it carries over from an earlier month.

Business first, a fifth card on every index. This setup reads the company before the chart. A stock qualifies where its company made a profit over its last four quarters together and in the latest one alone, the test S&P sets before adding a company to its indices; its latest quarter's sales grew on the same quarter a year before, and grew faster than the quarter before had; its gross or its operating margin widened on the year; and the cash its business brought in over its latest year was at least the profit it reported, a sign the profit is not mostly accounting entries. Then the chart has to agree: the price above its 200-day average and the 50-day above the 200-day. A stock passing all of that is bought only at the pullback's buy point, with the pullback's own stop and target. The business figures are the ones the company filed with the SEC, as first filed, and a filing counts from the first trading day after the day it was filed. Stocks whose sales came in above expectations have tended to keep rising after the report (Jegadeesh and Livnat 2006), more profitable companies have tended to earn more (Novy-Marx 2013), and profit backed by cash has lasted longer than profit that is not (Sloan 1996), though trading on that last difference has paid less since it was published (Green, Hand and Soliman 2011), so here it is a check on the profit and not a reason to buy. The drift after an earnings surprise is not used to time the buy, since it has faded outside the smallest companies. The card lists on the S&P 500, 400 and 600 from its first night on its starting settings, the wording above; a search over twenty-seven settings written down before it ran is brought to you, and nothing is frozen without your word.

Five a setup, and one trade a stock. Each setup lists at most five stocks a night in its own order, and a note counts any past its five. A stock two setups pass is listed once, under the first in the page's order, with a label naming the other. A stock with a trade still open from an earlier night is not listed again under any swing setup until that trade ends, and the card says so in a note. The sector heavyweights keep their own: a stock they hold can stand on a swing card too, and a swing trade holds back no heavyweight. That was one of two options put to you, and it stands until you rule on it.

Analysts raising their estimates, and the night's best three. The pullback has two more variants scored in the background. One is the same rule listing a stock only where analysts raised their estimate of its earnings for the current year over the past thirty days. The provider keeps no history of those estimates, so this variant was never swept: the night asks for the estimates of the one to three stocks it would list, stores each, and its record starts from its registration on 2026-10-04. It took the place of a variant reading leadership in place of trend and strength, a stock in the top quarter of one of the three strongest sectors, retired then because its sweep's result stood alone among its neighbours over a history missing many sectors. The other keeps the pullback's rule whole and lists only the night's first three in the list's own order, the highest reward to risk first. The ideas' run found it the one idea of its thirteen to pass its test, about as many as luck alone would pass, so it was not made the live rule; it was registered beside it instead, as the pullback's ninth rule, where its own record decides. Like every variant, neither lists anything on Tonight, and each record is kept beside the pullback's.

Seeing what a variant would have picked. Each card carries a small selector beside its state line: the live rule first, and then each variant by its number. Choose a variant and the card redraws as that rule saw the night, under a band saying it is scored in the background and not the live rule, and that its picks are not tonight's list; the words of its rule are written from its registered settings, with the clauses the live rule's words lack marked, so the one thing it changes stands out. The choice stays in the page's link beside the index chosen under Universe, and a link that names no choice shows the live rule. Beneath each card's picks are two more lines. The funnel counts how many stocks passed each of the rule's gates and every gate before it. The stretch line says how many nights the rule has listed nothing for, against a mark drawn from the rule's own past: the stretch that nineteen in twenty of its past empty nights had not gone past, counted night by night rather than stretch by stretch, since a long drought is one stretch however many nights it holds. The line is flagged past its mark, and until thirty stretches have completed over two years of sessions it says how far those floors are instead of drawing one. On the S&P 400 and 600 before a freeze the selector holds the provisional rule first, and says that no variant is registered where none is. Beside the pullback's, the breakout's and the drift's provisional rules on those two indices stands a variant that reads no market check, its words ending on the market check off. The check closes an index's lists while fewer than 45 in 100 of its members close above their 200-day average, a floor copied from the S&P 500 and never tested on the smaller indices, so the variant's own list and trades show what the check costs there; the page's list is still the provisional rule's. The sector heavyweights' card carries the same selector: choosing a variant there redraws the card from that rule's own book, the stocks it holds tonight, under the same band. The S&P 600's sector heavyweights carry two such books with no freeze, each kept beside the provisional rule and saying beside its band that it did not pass the test of each year it never saw, so neither reads as better than the rule the card is drawn by; one of them reads a year's strength from the oldest price the night keeps, a day short of the setting the search found.

Breakouts forming. Under each breakout card is a list of the stocks closest to breaking out: each within a move and a half of its look-back high, with the price it would have to close above, the volume the rule would need against tonight's, how far its ranges have narrowed, the gates it still fails and its next report where one falls within the next twenty sessions, the nearest misses first and ten at most. These have not broken out. If one closes above its breakout price on the volume shown, with its range under the rule's ceiling and the market check open, the rule reads its other gates that night as on any other, and lists five at most; on a night the market check closed, the list says so above its rows. Nothing on it is a pick.

The setup ledger. Behind the cards, each night also writes down every stock that came anywhere near a setup, not only the five a rule lists: every pullback, breakout and earnings drift that passes a much looser version of the rule's gates, on each of the three indices, and on the S&P 500 each of a sector's ten largest companies on the heavyweights' monthly rebalance, held as the rule would hold it. Each is stored with whether the live rule would have taken it, whether the night's list did, the plan it would have been bought on, forty-six readings of the stock, its business and the market as they stood that evening, and later how the trade would have turned out against the same plan entered on every stock in the index that night. One of those readings asks whether the stock is cheap or dear against its own past: its last year's earnings as a share of what the whole company is worth at that evening's price, set against the same figure at the end of each of its last twelve quarters, from the earnings and the share counts the company filed with the SEC. Three years of prices are more than the system keeps for each night, so for that one reading the night looks back into the older prices the operator pulled once, brought to the same scale. Nothing is read from a later day, so a row written tonight and a row rebuilt from the history for the same evening say the same thing. The ledger is the raw material the improvement loop reads: the proposals it will put to the operator are drawn from what these near-setups did, and never from a stock the rule happened to pick. The Ledger page, linked from the Universe page, shows each family's near-setups a year, how many the rule actually took, how the finished ones spread from worst to best, and any one of them drawn with its buy, stop and target.

The business readings and the filings refresh. Five of those readings describe the company rather than its chart: whether its sales grew against the same quarter a year before and whether that growth is speeding up, whether its gross and operating margins widened, and whether the cash its business brought in covers the profit it reported. They come from the figures companies file with the SEC, each as the company first filed it, and only from filings made before the evening the setup is read on, so a figure revised later never leaks back. To keep them fresh, after the close each night the tool reads the SEC's daily list of every filing it published, one free request a day, picks out the members that filed a quarterly or annual report, a correction to one, or an earnings announcement, and asks for those companies' figures alone. The SEC posts a day's list late in the evening, after the night has started, so a company that files today is refreshed tomorrow night, which is the first evening its new figures may be read anyway. A slow or unavailable SEC leaves the figures as they were and holds up nothing else.

Testing a change on years it never saw. A proposed change to a rule is only worth having if it would have helped in years nobody looked at when choosing it. The tester treats each year from 2022 as an exam: the search that proposes the change is run again using only the trades that had finished before that year began, whatever setting it picks is traded through that year, and the year's results are set against what the rule as it stands today did over the same year. A change passes only where four things hold together: the combined difference over all the exam years is too large to be luck, at a strict bar that stays the same every month; it was better in most of the complete years, the latest among them; it still helps once each side's five biggest results, good or bad, are set aside; and it rests on enough trades, or for the sector heavyweights, whose holdings last for months, enough months. The Loop page, linked from the Universe page, shows each family's rule as it is today and under it each proposal with those four tests and its exam years one by one. Nothing on the page changes a rule. Today's rules were themselves chosen with these same years in view, so the comparison leans in their favour, and the page says so.

Looking at how trades ended. The first thing the loop proposes is a better way out of a trade. For each family on each index it reads what the rule's own finished trades did between buying and selling: how far the typical trade rose and fell before it ended, how many days its best close took to come, how many trades that hit their stop had first been one risk up, and how far the trades that reached their target first dipped. Those figures explain; they never decide. The ways out it can propose come from a fixed list written before any trade was read: moving the stop up to the buying price once a trade is a little ahead, trailing the stop under the highest close, a nearer or further target, selling a trade that has not moved after a set number of days, or selling half at the target and trailing the rest; for the sector heavyweights, also selling a holding that closes under its long average, or rebalancing every week. Each of those is replayed exactly over every past trade and put through the same exam years as any other proposal, so the page shows a way out only with its test years beside it and the figures that suggested it. A rule's settings already have a place for each of these, switched off on every rule today, so an approved change would be a stored setting and never new code.

Setting winners against losers. The second thing the loop proposes is an extra condition a stock must meet before a rule lists it. For every stock a rule could have listed in the past, taken or not, it looks at the same measurements the ledger keeps, how stretched the stock was above its averages, how strong it had been, how quiet its trading was, and dozens more, and asks whether the stocks that went on to beat the market that day looked different from those that did not. It tries each measurement cut at ten points from low to high, either way round, and then a second measurement inside the best first one. Tried that many ways, something always looks like it separates winners from losers by luck, so every idea is checked against the same search run again with the winners and losers reshuffled among the stocks of each day: a condition goes forward only if it beats what the reshuffled search finds nineteen times in twenty. Shuffling within a day keeps the market's own ups and downs out of it, so a measurement that only says what kind of day it was cannot pass. Whatever passes is then put through the same exam years as any other proposal, chosen each year using only the years before. The Loop page shows each measurement's spread too: what winners and losers looked like on it, and how the results ran from its lowest tenth to its highest.

A learned score. The third thing the loop proposes is an order for a rule's list. It fits one sum over the same measurements, each weighted by how much it told the stocks that went on to beat the market from those that did not, across the near-setups of all three indices that had finished before the year being tested. Every weight is pulled toward nothing unless many setups support it, the biggest runaway results are clipped so a handful cannot set the weights, and the one measurement that peeks ahead in the history, the days to the next report, is left out. It proposes three things, each put through the same exam years: the rule's list ordered by the score, and the same with the lowest fifth or the lowest two fifths of scores left off. A score never lists a stock the rule's own gates refuse. It is worked out by code from stored figures and gives the same weights when fitted again on the same setups, so it counts as a rule and not as a judgment; no AI chooses or orders anything. The Loop page names the measurements it weighs most and how much of its weight the business measurements carry.

A second kind of score, the challenger. Beside that sum the loop fits a challenger on the same setups: a hundred small decision trees fitted by FastTree, a gradient boosting method from Microsoft's ML.NET library. Each tree asks a few yes-or-no questions of the measurements, such as whether a stock's recent strength is above a level, and adds a little to the score at the end of each path, and each is fitted to what the trees before it still got wrong. Trees can find a measurement that only matters together with another, which a single weighted sum cannot. The trees are fitted only when the tester runs, never on the night, and stored as plain text that the system walks itself, on the night as in the tests, so a stored set scores the same way every time it is read. An approved change that orders by the trees names the one stored set it reads, so trees fitted again next month change no rule. Their three proposals go through the same exam years, and one is put to you only where it passes and also earned more over those years than the weighted sum's proposal of the same kind, so the more complicated score has to earn its place against the simpler one; where the weighted sum has no proposal of that kind, the trees' is put to no one.

Setups like this one. On a pick's card, beneath the rule's record, a part shows how setups like tonight's pick went under the same rule on the same index: among the rule's finished setups, the 250 nearest on the measurements the score weighs most, never the pick's own stock and any one stock counted once in a month of trading, with their average result against the market that day and the range it would likely fall in. Beside it stands the rule's own average over all its finished setups, and where the range takes that in, which will usually be so, the card says the two cannot be told apart. It is a record of similar setups and not this stock's chance. The pick's place under the score is shown only once the score has passed its exams on that index; until then the card, the Loop page and the stock's page say the score is not yet validated on this index. The part and the place both read the weighted sum alone, never the trees.

Close to a buy point. Beneath the cards, one list across every setup shows the stocks one gate short of a setup's rule, each labelled with its setup and the gate it missed; none of them is a pick, and nothing counts them as one.

Why a filter and not a score. Each gate is a fact you can check on the chart. A single blended score would hide which fact put a stock on the list. The earlier design, where any one of six conditions listed a stock, let most of the index through every night, so the filter replaced it. Those six conditions are still shown beside each stock as context. A learned score, once it passes its exams and the operator approves it, may order the stocks a rule's gates pass and leave its weakest off, but it never puts a stock on the list.

Why separate setups. A pullback, a breakout and the drift after an earnings beat buy different moments for different reasons, so each keeps its own rule, its own five a night and its own record, and none is judged by another's. Stocks near their own recent high have tended to keep rising (George and Hwang 2004), and prices have tended to keep moving the way an earnings surprise moved them for weeks after the report (Bernard and Thomas 1989). The heavyweights are a different idea again, held for months rather than weeks: the industries that beat the others over six months have tended to keep beating them (Moskowitz and Grinblatt 1999), and a stock that swings more than the market is not part of the rule, since such stocks have earned less for their risk (Frazzini and Pedersen 2014).

7. How to read a pick's card

A stock is on tonight's list. What does its card tell me before I buy?

Each pick on Tonight has a card, opened beneath its row. It gathers what a decision on that one trade needs into eight parts, every figure in them worked out by the tool from stored prices and filings, never by a model. The picture below is a card with its figures blanked out as n.n and nn%, so it shows what each part says rather than any one night's numbers.

  1. Six quick checks on the trade. A tick means nothing stands against it, a "!" means look closer, and each says why in words: whether the stock is rising and strong, whether the company makes money and earns enough to pay its interest, whether the market and the stock's industry are healthy, whether the company reports its results before the trade would usually end, whether the stock is cheap to buy and sell, and how many of your open trades are in the same industry.
  2. How the rule that picked it has done. The rule replayed over years of past prices, after trading costs: how many trades it made, how many reached their target, the average gain or loss, and how long it held them. It is the rule's record over every stock it bought, never a forecast for this one.
  3. The trade in your money. Once you have entered your account's size and how much you are willing to lose on one trade, how many shares that buys and how much is at risk if the stop is hit.
  4. When to sell, exactly as the rule does. At the close of a day that ends below the stop, at the close of a day that ends at or above the target, or after a set number of days, whichever comes first.
  5. What could jolt the price before the trade ends. How far the stock moved on its past results days compared with this trade's stop, when its next dividend comes off the price, and the Federal Reserve decisions and inflation reports due in the meantime.
  6. Your own trades of this rule. How the trades you took from it went, and beneath them how the rule's own picks on those same days went at the plan's price, so a gap between the two is your choices and your buying prices rather than the market of those weeks.
  7. The button you press when you buy. It records the trade, at the price you paid if you enter it; until the next day's opening price is stored the card assumes the plan's price, and then that opening price.
  8. What is behind the stock. Its readings grouped under seven headings, from momentum to the quality of its earnings, each a bar of where it stands among the other members of its index that night, and a heading with nothing stored saying so. Where the learned score has passed its test on the index, each heading also shows how far it pushes the score up, in green, or down, in red, from a setup sitting at the averages to this one. It describes the stock; it is not the stock's chance.

Why a card and not a verdict. Each part is a fact you can check, and the card removes no pick and reorders none. Whether to buy stays your decision, made with the facts that bear on it in one place.

8. What a report contains

When I open a stock, what do I get, and where does each part come from?

A stock's page is a report on one company, laid out in the order someone deciding whether to buy would ask their questions: what the price is now and what has happened, how the business is doing, what the price has done, where buyers or sellers have stepped in before and what the plan does there, what could go wrong, and what is coming up. Nothing on it is a forecast or a recommendation. Code works out every number from figures it has stored. A language model writes a few sections in plain words, and every figure it writes is checked against the stored figures and the documents it cites; a section that fails the check is left out and the page says so. Each part shows the day its figures are from.

  1. The top bar. The stock's ticker symbol and the company's name, the index it belongs to and its sector, the broad part of the economy it is in, and its price. While the market is open the price is the data provider's, about fifteen minutes behind the market, marked LIVE with its time; outside market hours it is the last closing price.
  2. One sentence and four boxes. A sentence on what matters most about the stock now, with no figures in it, then four boxes: the company's latest profit for each share against what analysts expected, how fast analysts expect that profit to grow this year, or where no forecast is kept how much its sales over the last four quarters grew on the four before, its yearly dividend as a share of the price beside the government's ten-year bond, and how far the price is below its high of the past year.
  3. The contents. Every section below, numbered, in two columns. A section is marked NEW when it was written or filed since you last opened the page in this browser.
  4. How to read the page. What the page is for, the three things it will not do, which are predict the price, rank a company as an investment or say how much to buy, and what its words mean, folded shut until you open it.
  5. The short version. Three short paragraphs of at most five sentences each: what happened, what matters and what the plan does. A language model writes them last, from the sentences of the other written sections that passed the check, and may use no figure the four boxes already show.
  6. The decision card. Shown only when one of the system's rules picked the stock that night: six quick checks on the trade, how that rule has done on every stock it bought before, the trade in your money and what could jolt the price before the trade ends.
  7. The latest quarter. The company's last three months line by line: its sales, its profit at each stage, its profit for each share and the cash the business brought in, each against what analysts expected before the report and against the same three months a year before, with bars showing how sales and profit for each share grew over the last eight quarters.
  8. What management said. What the company's managers expect now against what they expected last time, and what they say is going well and badly, in their own words and checked word for word against their announcement.
  9. Segments. Where the sales come from: each of the company's business lines with its share of the whole and how it changed, set out this way only when the company's own figures for them add up to its total sales, and otherwise shown as its filing states them.
  10. Margins. How much of each sale the company keeps as profit at three stages, drawn as three lines over the last three years.
  11. Analysts. What the professional analysts who follow the company expect, how their forecasts have moved and how many rate it each way, every figure labelled as theirs and dated.
  12. Dividend safety. Only for a company that pays a dividend: whether its profit and its cash cover the payment, how many years in a row it has raised it, and how its yield compares with the government bond's.
  13. Valuation. What buyers pay for each unit of the company's profit, against its own past and against the S&P 500 companies in its industry.
  14. Earnings reactions. How far the price moved on each day the company reported its results, against how far it usually moves on such a day.
  15. The chart. The past year's prices with the price zones the system found shaded behind them, results days and dividend dates marked beneath, and the year's biggest moves numbered.
  16. The long view. The price at the end of every month over all the years the system holds, with the year's high and low and the plan's buying and selling zones drawn across it, so you can see whether the price has met those levels before.
  17. How it got here. The year's biggest price moves, each with what was behind it and where that explanation came from.
  18. Levels. The price zones above and below today's price where buyers or sellers have stepped in before, how far away each is in the stock's usual day's movement, and what the plan does at each.
  19. The plan. Where the plan buys, in equal parts, and where it sells, with what each part risks and what it could make for each unit it risks; at the top it stops naming a price and follows the price up with a rising stop, and a part with nothing beneath it to stop at states no risk. A stock with no plan says why, and what would bring one.
  20. How the rules read it. How each of the system's rules read the stock that night, folded shut.
  21. The risks. What could go wrong, each with what would show it happening, then two lists, what would turn the stock up and what would make it worse, each opening on a line worked out from the plan at the price shown: a close above the nearest zone of sellers, or a close below the price at which the plan sells everything.
  22. The two cases. The case for buying and the case against, at most six points each, written by the language model, with no figures of their own.
  23. The industry cycle. Where prices in the company's industry sit in their usual rise and fall, and what is pushing them.
  24. Its group, by price. The other companies whose prices move most like this one's.
  25. News. What was written about the company over the past thirty days, each article sorted by kind and by whether it is good or bad news for the company, the articles not yet read in a group of their own.
  26. Calendar. The next ninety days as a timeline and a table: the company's results, its next dividend, declared or estimated from how often it pays and drawn hollow when estimated, the Federal Reserve's rate decisions and the inflation reports, beside the dates the research found in the documents.
  27. Sources. Every document the written sections rely on, any figure two sources state differently, and what the code worked out itself rather than read from a document.
  28. Where the research stands. Whether the written sections are current, what writing them has cost, and the button that asks for a new report.

The colours. Down the left edge of each section from How to read the page on runs a coloured rule saying what kind of section it is: green for the decision card and the plan, the parts about buying; amber, with the word Caution above it, for the risks, drawn dashed in the picture above; a muted plum for a part a language model wrote; and grey for the rest. A written part and a part taken from a company's filing each carry a column at the left saying the day it was written or filed, since either may be weeks old. Inside the plan the rows where it buys are marked green, the rows where it sells orange and the stop red. A figure that rose is written in green and one that fell in red, on this page and every other, each with its plus or minus sign, so the sign says it to a reader who cannot tell the two colours apart.

Where the figures come from. The price, the zones and the plan come from the stock's daily prices, which the tool stores every night. The company's results come from what it filed with the SEC and from the data provider's copy of the same quarters; where the two state a line of the latest quarter differently by more than rounding, the sources list both, the filing's first, and the page draws the provider's, since every quarter it reads comes from that one source. The analysts' figures are the data provider's, labelled as theirs and dated by when they were fetched, and the government's ten-year bond yield is read each night from the Treasury.

What the numbers say. Under the latest quarter, a plain sentence for each of four readings of the company's reported quarters, written by code from a fixed pattern, says whether sales and the operating margin are growing on a year before, how the company has done against analysts' estimates, whether its profits are backed by cash and where its price sits in its own range of earnings multiples. The heading gives the company's state, improving, steady or deteriorating, and says it is read from sales and operating margin alone, so a business can read improving beside profits that ran ahead of its cash.

Looking back. A stock's usual move on a results day is the middle of its moves over every report since 2018, a fall and a rise of the same size counted alike, and the long view's monthly prices reach back to 2018 too, adjusted the way the chart's prices are. How it got here takes each move's cause from the written research where there is some, otherwise from the stories published during the move as the news labeller read them, otherwise from the results and filings that fell inside it, and says so where it found none. The news is labelled by a paid model you choose, and the page names which one wrote the labels.

Which setup listed it. A stock one of the rules picked says, beneath the contents, which setup listed it, its place on that setup's card and any other setup it qualified under, and a stock a setup passed but held back for a trade still open says so.

Saving a report. A report can be saved as one file to hand to someone. It opens in the light palette with a button for the dark one, draws every figure without running anything, and shows the price the page showed when it was saved, marked as such.

Why this order. The page follows the questions a person deciding whether to buy tends to ask, in the order they ask them: what the price is and what has happened, how the business is doing, what the price has done, where the plan would buy and sell, and then what could go wrong and what is coming up. The business comes before the chart because a price means little without what the company earns beside it, and the plan comes before the risks so each risk is read against a plan already on the page.

Why so little is written by a model. A figure worked out by code can be checked against the stored data it came from, and the page draws it exactly. Written words cannot be checked that way, so a model writes only what figures cannot say, such as what management said in its own words, the case for and against and the risks, and last the short version and the headline from the sentences that passed. The next section says how each is checked.

9. Research, written once and kept

How is the written analysis produced, and how do we know it is not made up?

How to read it. Most opens stop at the top right: nothing has changed, so the stored research is shown and nothing is spent. The lower path runs only when something has actually changed.

What to take from it. Nothing expires on a timer. Research is rewritten only when a filing, an earnings date, a news spike or you say so, and the night says so for the first six stocks its page lists.

How claims are checked. Every number in the writing must match a number the system computed or read from a filing. Every claim must name a document that was fetched and stored. A sentence about the system's own figures, such as the closing price or an average, marks them with [N] instead, and a sentence that names only documents may quote only numbers those documents contain, so a company's results release cannot be cited for a share price it never mentions. Each written part may state only the figures its own part of the page draws and says the rest in words, so a figure is drawn in one place with its source rather than repeated in prose. The headline and the short version are written last, from the sentences the check accepted in the other parts, each sentence citing the ones it rests on, and the short version may not repeat a figure the tiles show or run past three paragraphs of five sentences each, since the parts beneath draw every claim in full. A section that fails is asked for again up to three times, and one still failing is left out rather than guessed.

Why so strict. The failure of automated research is a confident sentence that is wrong. Requiring a stored source for every claim means any sentence can be traced back to the document that supports it, months later.

Why two models. Sorting and extracting from one document is small work a model on your own machine can do for free. Weighing all the evidence into a view of the company needs a stronger model, and that, with sorting a listed stock's news, is the part that costs money. The local model writes its part of a report when the report is written, from the company's own filing; nothing is drafted overnight, because the one section that was, a paragraph explaining the chart's figures, said what each figure's own key already says. Which local model it is, Gemma 4 from October 2026 with Qwen 3.5 kept beside it, is a setting: each model has its own entry and one is flagged as the one used, so you can switch between them to compare a day at a time, and every section records the model that wrote it. Before its first draft the tool loads the model and waits until it is ready, rather than failing while it loads.

When a section is refused. A refused section is told each thing the check refused and asked for again, up to three times, and one still refused at its last try is left out, the page naming each rule it was refused by. Where the short version or the headline is left out, or no other part was accepted to write it from, the page puts one written by code from the night's own figures in its place, saying so. Every model is told who the reader is, someone deciding whether to hold a swing trade over the next weeks to months, and is given each document marked as a company filing, a news report or someone's opinion.

Seeing which sections keep failing. The run page lists each report section by section: passed first time, passed on its retry, or left out and why, with what each cost, and each section's rate over the last twenty reports. One setting names the model for each section, so a section that keeps failing can be given to another model. Before that, a trial can ask the other model for the same section beside a few reports, and a command writes the drafts side by side to a file without changing the report or showing them on any page. For now every report is written by DeepSeek: the one report written on Claude cost many times what DeepSeek's did, so no setting names Claude.

10. How the picks get better

How does the system learn which settings work, without fooling itself?

How to read it. The loop runs every night for every stock, whether or not it made the list. That is how the system learns what the gates turned away, not only what they let in. The plan scored is the swing trade from section 6: bought at the close, with its stop and target taken from the bands around it. The stepped plan in section 5 is what the report shows you. You can follow every recommended trade on the Past picks screen.

What to take from it. Nothing changes the list without your decision, and every change is written down with its evidence.

A worked example, with illustrative numbers. A plan buys at 100, stops at 95 and targets 112. The risk is 5 and the reward is 12, so the plan comes out even if it wins 5 times in every 17, about 29% of the time. A setting earns its place only if its plans win more often than their own break-even, and more often than a plan with no skill would from the same starting point.

What the loop can change

On the S&P 500 the shape clock proposes moves to three of the pullback's filter settings: how strong a stock's recent return must be, how quiet the volume must be on the way down, and the minimum reward for the risk. Each move is tested like any other proposal, on years it never saw, and a move you approve opens a new filter version from the next night. The pullback, the breakout and the drift can also take an exit, an extra condition or a score order you approve on the Loop page, on the index you approve it on. How deep a pullback counts, whether the market gate applies at all, how far away the stop may sit and how healthy the market must be move only by your ruling. The rules for finding levels and building the report's plan do not change through this loop.

Two clocks, answering two different questions

How to read it. The shape clock proposes moves to those settings so each gate lets through a sensible number of stocks, and each move is tested before it is put to you. The edge clock runs a few alternative versions of each setup's rule in the background, each differing in one setting, and shows at its checkpoints which version's trades actually do better than they need to, each setup against a bar of its own. What it shows is monitoring: the list changes only through a proposal tested on years it never saw and approved by you.

What to take from it. The list's size can be tuned within months. Whether the picks make money takes years to answer with confidence, and the page says so plainly so an empty result reads as waiting, not broken.

Why so slow. Try enough settings and one will look good by luck. Studies of trading rules show that most apparent winners disappear once you account for how many were tried (Sullivan, Timmermann and White 1999; White 2000; Harvey, Liu and Zhu 2016). So every alternative is registered before it is measured, measured in the background where it cannot influence the list, and judged against a stricter bar when several are tested at once (Holm 1979).

Why years rather than a count of trades. Trades that overlap in time share the same market, so they are not independent pieces of evidence. The system counts evidence in blocks of time rather than in trades (Cameron and Miller 2015).

Why fixed checkpoints. Looking at results again and again and stopping when they look good finds false winners. Results are judged only at set points, the way medical trials handle the same problem (Armitage, McPherson and Rowe 1969; Lan and DeMets 1983; FDA 2019).

Why a limit on adjustments. Each time the live settings change, the edge clock has to start again. So after the first adjustment, any further change shows how much evidence it would throw away before you approve it.

Why the turned-away stocks count. Each gate is also judged by what it turned away. If the stocks a gate rejects would have done as well as the ones it lets through, that gate is not earning its place, and the page says so.

Where the starting settings come from

The settings the list starts from were first chosen by hand. The sweep replaces that guess with a starting point the stored history supports: it replays the filter over the years of history the store holds under tens of thousands of designs, each a different way of measuring strength, reading the trend, finding support, spotting the trigger and placing the trade, and under every combination of the settings, and scores every trade each would have taken the way the live record scores them, with one open trade a stock as the list holds it. Each result is read as an edge over what the same trade, placed on every stock in the index that night, would have come to, so a setting is not credited for a rising market. Seven further conditions, such as the stock sitting near its 52-week high or a recent earnings beat, are each tried alone and kept only where they help on most of the strongest designs, and before anything is ranked a sample of nights is rebuilt with the night's own code to show the sweep read each night as it stood. It does not pick the single best result, because among that many tries one always looks good by luck. It looks for a plateau instead, a region of settings that all beat the odds year after year, and proposes the setting sitting deepest inside it, the one that can be moved furthest in any direction and still hold. Beside it, it proposes a few variants, each changing one thing on a question the history left open and picking stocks the starting point would not. None of it takes effect by itself: the sweep writes a report, and the starting point and its variants begin running only when you register them, after which only the trades that follow are judged. When the checkpoints show a variant ahead, the sweep can be run again with the added years to choose the next open questions, and a change it suggests reaches the list only as a proposal tested on years it never saw and approved by you, so the loop keeps going.

The breakout and the drift each have a sweep of their own, which takes minutes, since a setup's grid holds tens of settings and not millions: its own rule replayed over the history at every setting, five a night with one open trade a stock, each trade read as an edge over the same plan placed on every stock that night. You froze all three setups on 2026-10-02: the pullback at its settings with the minimum reward for the risk raised to 2, and the breakout and the drift at their sweeps' proposals, each first checked against the settings one step away so that neither is a point the history happened to favour. Each was registered with its variants beside it, and only the trades after the freeze count toward its record.

The ideas' run then tests kinds of idea the sweep's grid does not hold, each added to the pullback alone: switches that keep the list off a weak market, a stop touched during the day, only the night's best three, a trailing stop, a stop no closer than a typical move, and shorter holds. An idea is kept only where it does better than the pullback in at least six of the eight years with two of the last three, and stays better without its largest few results. A market switch is judged on each year's total result instead, since the edge already subtracts what the whole market did on the nights it keeps off. The report says how many ideas were tried and how many luck alone would pass, and nothing it proposes reaches the list until you freeze it.

The same ideas are read on the breakouts and the earnings drift as each was frozen, one setup a run and by the same test, with the two trailing stops tried on the drift alone, since the breakout's stop already trails. What they show is a report and nothing more: a setup's rule changes only when you freeze it again.

From those two reports you registered one switch on each setup as a variant on 2026-10-03: the breakout listing only on nights the index closes above its 200-session average, and the drift only on nights the VIX closes under its close ten sessions before. Neither passed the test, each failing on the yearly totals a switch is judged on, since a night it sits out counts nothing, the breakout's on its count of trades as well, and the drift's lowering its plain result a trade from 0.278 to 0.241, its edge rising only because the same plan placed on every stock did worse on the nights it kept, so their own records weigh them from their first night. To read them the night asks for the index's and the VIX's daily closes, one request each, and a night it cannot read a switch on lists nothing under that variant.

How a result reaches tonight's list

The pullback's filter runs every night on a stored settings version, and each setup's rule on the settings it was frozen at. The feedback never edits those settings itself. It prepares a change, the tester tests it on years it never saw, the Loop page puts it to you, and the change reaches the list only when you approve it.

How to read it. Blue boxes run on their own. Teal boxes are what you see and do. The only route to the list passes through "You decide".

What to take from it. The system does the measuring and the proposing. Changing the list is always one deliberate step by you, and both the old and the new settings stay on record.

How EquityBrief gets better

Since phase 17 the system keeps a ledger of every setup a family's loose gates pass on each index, picked or not, with its readings as they stood and how it turned out. Once a month it reads that ledger and each rule's own trades, proposes changes, tests each on years it never saw, and puts at most one change a family on each index to you. Nothing changes a rule until you approve it, and each index is judged and changed apart from the others. On the S&P 500 an approved change to the breakout or the drift also becomes the family's registered live rule on the night it is applied, so its record starts that night, and the rule it replaced keeps running beside it as a variant with its record kept for one block of 63 sessions before it retires, unless you approve a restore first. An approved change to the S&P 500's pullback, a way out, a condition or an order, is stored and read by the pullback's card from the night it is applied, and an approved move of one of its filter settings opens the filter's next settings version that night. What the loop proposes, and the two scores it fits, the weighted sum and its challenger of decision trees, are described in section 6. The four figures below follow one night, the test years, one month, and one month across the three indices.

How to read it. One night, along the top row and then the bottom one. Green boxes are arithmetic, blue boxes are stores the night writes, and the purple box reads from outside. The families draw the lists, and then the ledger appends the setups: every member and night a family's loose gates pass, kept or not, with its readings as they stood that night. Windows close as paths end, so a setup's result and its edge over the same plan on every member are written once its trade would have ended. The filings refresh reads the SEC's archive after the close for the members that filed and for no other, their business readings read again from the new filings, and the summary rewritten for the Ledger page.

What to take from it. The ledger is the loop's memory. It holds the setups the rules turned away as well as the ones they picked, so a change can be judged on everything it would have done.

How to read it. Each row is one test year: Tests 2022, Tests 2023, Tests 2024, Tests 2025 and Tests the newest, the months since the last complete year. The green bar is what a proposal is built from, every setup and trade that ended before its year began. The orange bar is the year it is judged on, which it never saw, its trades against the rule's own over the same year. The dashed bar is drawn and tested, and it does not count toward the years a proposal must be better in.

What to take from it. A proposal is never judged on the data it was built from, so a change that only fits the past fails here.

How to read it. One month, along the top row and then the bottom one. The ledger and the rules' trades are what the nights stored. The engines propose: the autopsy an exit, winners against losers a condition, and the learned score, a weighted sum with a challenger of decision trees beside it, an order or a floor. The tester judges each proposal against the rule today on the years the proposal never saw. The Loop page draws what the tester stored and computes nothing, the orange box is you: you approve or decline the one proposal put to you for each family, and with your approval the change applied is written as a stored setting before the next night.

What to take from it. The system measures and proposes. Only you change a rule, one change a family a month at most.

How to read it. Each column is one index for one month: the large caps are the S&P 500, the mid caps the S&P 400 and the small caps the S&P 600, each with its own setups and its rules' own trades. Each index's proposals are tested on its years, against that index's own rules today. Its own Loop page draws them, and your word on one index is given there alone. An approved change is applied to it alone. On the S&P 400 and 600 it is a stored setting read from the next night. On the S&P 500 it is applied by the night: a breakout's or a drift's change is stored and registered as the family's live rule on the first night whose session a new registration can count from, the rule it replaced running beside it for 63 sessions; a change to the pullback's way out, condition or order is stored for the pullback's card from the next night; and a move of one of the pullback's filter settings opens the filter's next settings version on a night a registration can count from.

What to take from it. Nothing crosses from one column to another. A change proven on one index says nothing about another, and approving it touches no other index's rule.

A worked example: an autopsy finding and its change, on one index, with placeholders in place of figures. On the S&P 600 the autopsy reads every breakout trade the rule took over the history: the median trade's best close came n.n risks above its buy before its trailing stop ended it, and nn% of the trades the stop ended had first closed a full risk up. So it proposes the exit from its menu that keeps the most of that, a target at n.n risks. The tester runs that same choice inside each test year, on the trades that ended before the year began, and scores what it picks on the year itself: better in n of n counted years, still ahead with the five largest results set aside, and an adjusted p-value under the bar the month's run is held to. The month's report puts it to you as the breakout's one proposal on the S&P 600. You approve it on the Loop page, and before the next night the S&P 600's breakout reads that exit. The S&P 400's breakout, tested apart, stands as it was.

A worked example: a proposal passing on the S&P 600 and not carried to the S&P 500. Winners against losers finds that, on the S&P 600's drift, the setups bought while the VIX closed under n.n did better than the rest, beyond nn% of the within-night shuffles of its own search, so it proposes that condition, and it passes on the S&P 600's test years. The same search on the S&P 500's own history finds another cut or none, and is tested there on its own, since one index's result says nothing of another's. Approved on the S&P 600, the change is applied to the S&P 600's drift alone. The S&P 500's drift stands as it was, and changes only by a proposal of its own that passes on the S&P 500's test years and that you approve there.

A worked example: a proposal rejected on the years it never saw. The learned score is fitted on the setups of all three indices that ended before each test year, and proposed on the S&P 400's pullback as an order for its list, strongest first. On the years it learned on it looked strong. On the test years it was better in only n of n, and with the five largest results set aside its total fell under nothing. It did not pass: the Loop page draws it with each of its four parts and the one that failed, no press is offered on it, and the month's report puts nothing to you for the family. The rule stands as it was, and the score is fitted again next month.

11. The screens

Where do I look, and what does each page answer?

How to read it. Tonight and the universe are for finding stocks. Tonight opens on a line about the market, whether it left the lists open and how many stocks the setups list, then a card for each setup, and beneath the cards one list across the setups of the stocks one gate short of a buy point, there to be watched and never counted as picks. A report is for reading one. The run page is the evidence behind everything else, and Past picks is the record of every trade the list recommended, which you can filter by setup; a breakout's trade has no target and is drawn with its result in multiples of its risk. A stock holds one open trade at a time across every setup: while a trade the list recommended is still open, a night a setup passes the stock again is shown as a note on that setup's card and marked on Past picks rather than counted as a second trade. The run page draws each setup with the day its rule went live, its variants and how far each rule's record has come toward its first checkpoint, and compares tonight's list with what each version of the pullback's rule running in the background would have picked: those picks are shown there and nowhere else, and how their trades turn out stays hidden until the version's checkpoint, so no version is kept or dropped on a lucky month. The breakout's and the drift's rules each show their trades and the edge of those already decided as they come in, while what the edge means waits for the checkpoint. The queue page lists every report asked for, the one being written and what came of the rest, with when each will start and end, and says so where the drain that writes them stopped on an error, until a later drain starts a report; the watch list holds up to twenty stocks you choose to follow, each as it stands on the newest night. A night that stops partway tries again by itself from the step that stopped, up to three more times fifteen minutes apart, and tonight's page and the run page say whether it is running, waiting to try again or left unfinished, with a button that runs the rest of it.

What to take from it. On every screen green means a level below the price, a buy or a gain; red means a stop, a loss or something that failed; orange means a level above the price or a sale; and amber means take care or wait. Each setup has a colour of its own too, drawn as the edge of its tile and the small square beside its name and never as the colour of words, and its name always stands beside it.

The top of tonight's page. Every page opens on one line: the tool's name, the page's title, three tabs choosing the index the page reads, and a menu holding the other pages, the search, the light and dark switch and the report lane's switch. Tonight then shows the date and, before the night has run, when it will; whether the market left the lists open; a tile for each setup with tonight's count; and one table of every buy point across the setups. Pick a row and its plan and its card open just beneath it. The time the night took, what research cost and how the code is checked moved to the run page.

  1. One line across the top. The tool's name, the page's title, three tabs choosing the S&P index the page reads, and the menu, whose button says which report lane is on and whose panel holds the other pages, the Loop with a count of the changes waiting for your word, the search and the switches.
  2. The date. The evening the page shows, and before tonight's run has started, the time it will start, read off when the last several nights started.
  3. The market line. Whether the market left the lists open, how many stocks passed a setup and how many buy points there are tonight.
  4. A tile for each setup. Tonight's count and a word on why, listing, held back by a trade still open, forming or nothing tonight, or for the heavyweights how many sessions until they next rebalance. Press one to jump to that setup's card.
  5. Every buy point in one table. Each row tagged with its setup, numbered from the top, with the price to buy at, the stop, the target and how much it could gain against what it risks.
  6. The row you pick. Its plan and its card open in a row just beneath it, the card ending on what is behind the stock; the first row is picked until you choose another.
