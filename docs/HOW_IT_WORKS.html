1. What it is, and what it will not do

What problem does this tool solve?

Every evening after the US market closes, EquityBrief reads every stock in three indices: the S&P 500 of the largest companies, the S&P 400 of mid-sized ones and the S&P 600 of smaller ones, about fifteen hundred stocks in all. For each index it finds the few stocks sitting at a buy point for a swing trade, a position held for days to a few weeks, and lists them on that evening's page.

The picks come from five setup families. Each is a rule of its own with its own card, its own list of at most five stocks a night and its own record: the pullback into support, the breakout to a new high, the drift after an earnings beat, the short-term dip, which buys a sharp drop in an uptrend and sells the bounce within days, and the sector heavyweights, a rotation among the largest companies held for months. Business first, which read the company before the chart, ran among them until 30 October 2026. Beside them, Quality at a fair price holds the ten companies whose filings score strongest at a price no higher than their own usual, reshuffled once a month. Each index runs its own copy of every family, judged among that index's own stocks, so a rule that works on the largest companies is never assumed to work on the smallest.

Rules choose the stocks, and no model ever judges a stock. Every pick, and the order it is listed in, comes from fixed rules over stored prices, company filings, counts and dates. A rule can be replayed over years of stored history and gives the same answer every time, so its record can be measured. One more kind of input may join them: the answers a local model on your own machine gives to four closed questions about a company's results release, read with its names and dates removed, under a pin that makes it answer the same text the same way every time (section 10), which the earnings drift's tone variant reads (section 6). A language model writes the research reports and sorts the news, and nothing it writes there decides, filters, ranks or orders a pick, a list, a gate or a record.

Behind the lists the system keeps three kinds of evidence. Each rule's record counts its own trades after their costs, set against the same plan bought at the next session's open on every stock of the index, or for a heavyweight against its sector's largest companies. Paper trading turns each rule's trades into an account of the size you choose, so the rules can be compared in money. And once a month the improvement loop proposes changes to the rules, tests each on years it never saw, and puts the strongest to you. No rule changes without your approval.

Any stock in the three indices can also be opened on its own. It opens on its chart, and from there to a full report: the prices that matter, a plan, the company's numbers and a written view of the business.

It does not predict prices.It finds prices a stock has respected before and says what to do if it gets there again.
It does not rank companies as investments.It judges setups on the chart. What it says of a business, whether its sales and margins are growing, is read from the company's reported quarters by fixed rules.
It does not trade.It places no order and holds no position. Paper trading and every record are worked out from stored prices, and the trades you take are the ones you record yourself.
It does not choose your position's size.It says where to buy and sell. A pick's card works out shares only from the account size, the risk a trade and the position cap you set on Paper trading, and the written report sizes nothing.
It spends money only where you have said it may.The nightly arithmetic is free. Written research costs money, for six stocks a night taken in turn from the three indices' pages and for any you ask for, and so does sorting the news of the S&P 500's listed stocks, each kept inside limits you set.

Why these limits. A forecast or a ranking would need evidence the tool does not have. Levels, plans and their outcomes can each be checked, so the tool keeps to what can be checked.

2. One night, in one picture

What happens after the close, what happens once the dearer hours end, and what happens when you read?

How to read it. Blue is the night, which is arithmetic only: one request fetches the day for every stock in the three indices, then the levels, the plans, each family's list on each index and the ledger of near-setups are worked out and stored. Purple is the paid work. It runs five minutes after each of the research model's dearer hours ends, at 04:05 and 10:05 UTC every day, so a weekday night's reports are written by the small hours in New York and a Friday's on the Saturday: first the news of the S&P 500's listed stocks is sorted, then the six reports the night asked for are written. Teal is what you see, all of it read from what was stored.

What to take from it. Everything you look at was worked out the night before. Money is spent only in the purple boxes, and only inside your limits.

Why split it this way. About fifteen hundred stocks every night must be cheap and fast, so the night itself calls no AI model and asks for the whole market's day in one request rather than one a stock. Written analysis is slow and costs money, so it runs as work of its own once the dearer hours end, for the few stocks the night lists first and for the ones you ask for, and it is kept. Each paid job stops at its own time and money limits, and what a stop leaves waits for the next run.

A night that stops. A night that stops partway tries again by itself from the step that stopped, up to three more times fifteen minutes apart. Tonight's page and the Run page say whether it is running, waiting to try again or left unfinished, with a button that runs the rest of it.

A copy of the store every night. Once the night has finished, the whole store is copied into a folder of its own. Each copy is opened and read back against the store before it counts, and the newest three are kept, so a damaged night still leaves the two before it. The Run page says when the newest copy was made and where it is. A copy guards against a damaged file; one kept on the same disk does not guard against the disk failing.

Twice a day, the local model's runs. At 12:05 and 21:40 UTC a run of its own fetches the results releases companies filed since the run before, free from the SEC, and the local model on your machine reads the week's releases with their names and dates removed. It never runs inside the night, holds none of the night's locks and spends nothing, and the Run page shows each run.

Once a month. On the first Saturday of each month, after Friday's night, the improvement loop runs. It refreshes the companies' filings, rebuilds each index's history of near-setups, checks that nothing in it read a later day, asks the local model for its ideas, tests its proposals and every rule standing, and writes the month's report. Section 10 explains it.

3. Words used in this guide

The few terms everything else depends on.

Session
One trading day.
Index
The S&P 500, 400 or 600. Every page reads one index at a time, chosen in the bar at its top, and every count on it names that index.
Support
A price where the stock has stopped falling before.
Resistance
A price where the stock has stopped rising before.
Band
A support or resistance level drawn as a narrow price range, because prices a few cents apart cannot be told apart.
Swing high, swing low
A local peak or trough: a day higher, or lower, than the days around it.
Moving average
The average closing price over the last 20, 50 or 200 sessions.
Retracement
A price a set fraction of the way back between the last swing high and swing low.
Volume shelf
A price range where an unusually large share of recent trading happened.
Typical daily move
How far this stock moves on an ordinary day. Used as a ruler, so "two typical days away" means the same for a calm stock and a volatile one.
Trend label
Uptrend, range or downtrend, read from the moving averages and the swings.
Pullback
A dip back toward support after a rise.
Tranche
One step of a purchase made in steps.
Stop
The price at which a trade is sold because it was wrong. Here it means a close below that price, not a touch.
Invalidation
The price below which the whole idea is wrong.
Risk, and a result in risks
The distance from the buy to the stop. A trade that gains twice that distance made two risks, so trades of different prices can be added up.
Reward to risk
The distance to the target divided by the distance to the stop.
Break-even rate
How often a plan must reach its target before its stop just to come out even.
Breadth
The share of the index trading above its own 200-day average. A reading of the whole market's health.
Relative strength
How a stock's recent return compares with every other stock in the index.
Setup family
One kind of buy point with a rule of its own: the pullback, the breakout, the earnings drift, business first, the short-term dip or the sector heavyweights. Each lists at most five stocks a night on each index.
Provisional
A family's rule on an index before it is frozen there: it lists from its first night on starting settings, and its card says so.
Freeze
Fixing a family's settings on an index, on your word, from what its test over the history found. It registers the live rule and its variants, and only trades from then on count toward its record.
Variant
A registered rule run beside the live one, usually differing from it in one setting. It runs every night with its own list, trades and paper account. It never draws Tonight's list, though its tab on the card shows what it would have picked.
Edge
How much better a trade did than the same plan bought at the next session's open on every stock in the index, or for a heavyweight than its sector's largest companies, so a rising market earns a rule nothing.
Record
A rule's trades after their costs, with their edge. It is the rule's record over every stock it bought, never one stock's chance.
Settings version
The set of thresholds the pullback's filter runs with each night. A change opens a new version; the old one and its record are kept.
Ledger
Every near-setup the families' looser gates pass on every index, picked or not, with its readings as they stood and how it would have turned out. The improvement loop's memory.
Test years
The years from 2022 on, each used once as an exam for a proposed change that never saw it while being chosen.
Release reading
One of four closed answers the local model gives about a company's results release, read with its names and dates removed: results better, in line, worse or not stated; outlook raised, kept, lowered or withdrawn, or none given; tone confident, neutral or cautious; demand stronger, steady, weaker or not stated. Code turns each answer into a number, not stated or none given counting as no reading. The local model gives them, each kept under the pinned version it was read under, and no rule reads one yet.
Learned score
An order for a rule's list fitted by code to the setups of the past: a weighted sum, and beside it a challenger of decision trees. It never lists a stock its rule's gates refuse.
Paper account
One rule's trades walked through an account of your size, paying their costs, worked out each time the page is read and stored nowhere.

4. Finding the prices that matter

Where has this stock repeatedly stopped falling or rising?

How to read it. The line is the price over a few months. Orange bands sit above the price, green bands below it. Each open circle is a day the price turned at a band.

What to take from it. A band that turned the price several times is stronger evidence than one that turned it once.

Four kinds of evidence become one band

How to read it. Each box on the left is one reason a price might matter. Where several reasons land close together, they become one band.

What to take from it. A band backed by several kinds of evidence, touched often and recently, scores highest.

Why levels at all. Support and resistance levels have been found to mark where prices pause more often than chance would give, and to sit where buy and sell orders cluster (Osler 2000; Chung and Bellotti 2021; Kavajecz and Odders-White 2004).

Why volume shelves. Where many shares changed hands, many holders have that price as their cost. They tend to defend it on the way down and sell near it on the way up.

5. Turning those prices into a plan

If I wanted to buy this stock, where would I buy, where would I be wrong, and where would I sell?

How to read it. One vertical price scale. The dark line is tonight's close. Green is where to buy, orange is where to sell, dashed lines are stops, red is where the idea fails.

What to take from it. Every number in a plan comes from a band on the chart, so every instruction can be traced to a price the stock has respected before.

The trend label decides the plan's shape

How to read it. An uptrend means the price is above its 50 and 200-day averages with each swing low higher than the last. A downtrend is the reverse. Anything else is a range.

What to take from it. The same bands give a different plan depending on the trend, and a falling stock is never given a buying plan.

Why buy in steps. A support band is a zone, not a single price, and nobody knows where in it the price will turn. Spreading the purchase across bands means being partly right is still useful (Brennan, Li and Torous 2005).

Why stops on a close. Prices dip through levels during the day far more often than they close through them. A stop on a touch is taken out by ordinary noise; a stop on a close is taken out by a real decision. Overnight gaps can still jump past a stop, which is why the report states the gap risk around earnings (Arratia and Dorador 2019).

Why no position sizes in the report. How much to buy depends on your money and your tolerance for loss, so the report's plan sizes nothing. A pick's card turns its plan into shares only from the account size, risk and position cap you set on Paper trading.

6. Choosing tonight's lists

Out of about fifteen hundred stocks, which are at a buy point tonight?

Each index's page is drawn from the families in one order: the pullback, the breakout, the earnings drift, Fundamentals, the short-term dip, and then the sector heavyweights. Business first stood in Fundamentals' place until 26 October 2026, ran out of sight until 30 October 2026, and runs no longer. Each family reads every member of the index every night and lists at most five in its own order. Each is drawn below the same way: its gates as a funnel, a table of what each gate asks and why it is there, and how its trade is bought and sold. Quality at a fair price follows them, a table of what it asks and how it holds, its card after the sector heavyweights'.

The pullback

The pullback buys a rising stock that has dipped back into support on quiet trading, at a price its own chart has respected before, once its buy signal fires.

How to read it. Each layer is one test. A stock must pass every layer to reach the bottom.

What to take from it. Each card is short on purpose, and some nights every card is empty. The market gate closes every swing setup's card at once, and the line at the top of the page says whether the market left the lists open; the sector heavyweights' card is not closed by it.

GateWhat it asksWhy it is there
The marketIs a large enough share of the index above its long-term average?Buying dips works poorly when most stocks are falling together.
Trend and strengthIs this stock rising, and has it done better than most others lately?Stocks that have outperformed over recent months have tended to keep outperforming (Jegadeesh and Titman 1993; George and Hwang 2004).
A setupHas it pulled back into support from a recent high, on volume that dried up on the way down?Short-term dips tend to partly reverse (Jegadeesh 1990; Lehmann 1990), and a real level gives a clear place to be wrong. Breakouts have a family of their own, below.
A fresh triggerDid the buy signal fire in the last few sessions?The list shows moments, not stocks that have been sitting at a price for weeks.
A trade worth takingFrom tonight's price, is the target far enough away compared with the stop?A good chart with a poor payoff is not a trade. Measuring from tonight's price also drops any stock that has already run away from its buy point.
ExclusionsIs an earnings report due within the holding period? Are the prices in doubt?An earnings report can jump straight past a stop (Dubinsky and others 2019).

The exact values each gate uses are in the specification. They change only through a tested proposal you approve, described in section 10.

The trade. Its plan is set at the night's close and it is bought at the next session's open. Its stop is the low edge of the support band it pulled back into, or of the next support band beneath it where that edge sits less than a typical day's move below, and its target is the lowest band at least two typical moves above. It ends at the stop, at the target or at its cap on holding time.

Improving businesses first. Among the stocks that pass, the pullback's card puts first those whose business is improving: sales above a year earlier and the operating margin wider than a year earlier, in both of the last two quarters. Steady businesses come next, then those with too few quarters or none stored yet, then those deteriorating, and inside each group the order stays reward to risk, then strength, then support. Every stock that passes is still listed. The order decides what comes first, not what is recommended, and no gate reads the business at all.

The breakout

The breakout buys a stock the day it breaks to a new high on heavy volume, after a quiet stretch, and rides it for as long as it keeps climbing.

How to read it. Each layer is one test, read as the pullback's is. The settings shown are the S&P 500's, frozen from its sweep. The S&P 400 and 600 each run the breakout frozen at the setting their own search chose, held to their price, trading and profit floors as well: on the S&P 400 a new high of a whole year with no limit on the ranges and the market check read on the S&P 500's share, and on the S&P 600 a half year's high on a quarter more than the usual volume with the market check at 40 in 100 of its own members.

What to take from it. A breakout needs all four on the same night, so most nights the card lists few stocks or none, and its stocks closest to breaking out are listed beneath it.

GateWhat it asksWhy it is there
The marketIs a large enough share of the index above its long-term average?On the S&P 500 every swing family reads the same market check, so a weak market closes them together; on the S&P 400 and 600 each frozen rule reads the check its own search chose.
A new highDid it close above its highest price of the past half year?Stocks near their own recent high have tended to keep rising (George and Hwang 2004).
Heavy volumeWas tonight's volume at least one and a half times its fifty-day average?A new high on ordinary volume is a price nobody was pushed to pay, and unusually heavy trading has tended to come before gains (Gervais, Kaniel and Mingelgrin 2001).
A quiet run-upWere its daily ranges over the last twenty sessions no wider than 0.85 of the twenty before?A range that tightened before the break is the base breakout traders look for. No study sets the figure: the family's sweep proposed it and you froze it, and the setting it replaced, ranges merely no wider, runs on beside it as a variant.

The trade. Its plan is set at the night's close and it is bought at the next session's open. Its stop starts one and a half typical moves below the close and trails the highest close since the buy at the same distance, raised as the price climbs and never lowered. It has no target, so a winner is held while it keeps climbing, and it ends at the first close under the stop or after 63 sessions. Its result is read in multiples of the risk its plan set. Trailing stops have been found to cut the worst losses at the cost of a lower average result (Dai, Marshall, Nguyen and Visaltanachoti 2021).

Breakouts forming. Under each breakout card is a list of the stocks closest to breaking out: each within a move and a half of its look-back high, with the price it would have to close above, the volume the rule would need against tonight's, how far its ranges have narrowed, the gates it still fails and its next report where one falls within the next twenty sessions, the nearest misses first and ten at most. These have not broken out. If one closes above its breakout price on the volume shown, with the market check open, the rule reads its other gates that night as on any other. Nothing on it is a pick.

The earnings drift

The earnings drift buys a company in the few days after it beat its estimate and the market cheered, on the evidence that prices keep moving the way a surprise moved them.

How to read it. Each layer is one test. The reaction day is the first session to trade on the report. The settings shown are the S&P 500's, frozen from its sweep. The S&P 400 and 600 each run the drift frozen at the setting their own search chose, held to their own floors as well; on the S&P 400 within three sessions of a reaction of one and a half typical moves on twice the usual volume, its target at twice the risk and the market check at 50 in 100 of its own members; on the S&P 600 within three sessions of the reaction, held up to 63 sessions, with no market check, and only where the quarter the report covers sold more than the same quarter a year before, both figures as first filed with the SEC and stored before the night.

What to take from it. The drift lists only in the few days after each report, so its card is often empty outside earnings season.

GateWhat it asksWhy it is there
The marketIs a large enough share of the index above its long-term average?On the S&P 500 every swing family reads the same market check; on the S&P 400 and 600 each frozen rule reads the check its own search chose.
A beatDid the newest report beat the analysts' estimate?Prices have tended to keep moving the way an earnings surprise moved them for weeks after the report (Bernard and Thomas 1989).
A strong reactionDid the stock close up at least half a typical move on its reaction day?The market's own first verdict on the report. The day's return has been found to carry more about the drift to come than the size of the beat alone (Brandt, Kishore, Santa-Clara and Venkatachalam 2008).
Heavy volumeWas the reaction day's volume at least twice its fifty-day average?A reaction on ordinary volume is a price few were pushed to pay.
Still holdingIs tonight within three sessions of the reaction day, counting that day, and still above its low?The drift is steepest in the first days after a report, and a close back under the reaction day's low says the market changed its mind.

The trade. It is listed at the close of the reaction day or either of the two sessions after it, and bought at the next session's open. Its stop is the reaction day's low, and its target the nearer of the first price band at least two typical moves above the close and two and a half times the risk above it. It ends at the stop, at the target or after 60 sessions, about the span the drift was first measured over. On the S&P 400 and 600 its card also states the published evidence against it: the drift after a surprise has faded in recent years (Martineau 2022), and where it lasted, in the least traded stocks, trading costs took most of it.

The tone variant. One variant of the drift on each index lists a setup only where the results release behind the report reads better than expected and its outlook was not lowered or withdrawn, in the local model's answers to the release read with its names and dates removed (section 10), under the reading version the rule names. The release is the newest one the night may read that the SEC accepted from the start of the session before the report date, so a release from an earlier quarter never answers for this one. A setup whose release holds no answer under that version lists nothing under the variant, and the card's funnel shows how many reached a release read and how many passed its tone. The evidence is that the drift after a report is larger where the words of the announcement read strong (Meursault, Liang, Routledge and Scanlon, PEAD.txt). On the S&P 500 it is the live drift rule with the condition, the family's ninth rule; on the S&P 400 and 600 it is the provisional rule with it. It is registered once the history has been read under its version, and its paper account and record run from its registration. The monthly per-rule test walks it over the history as it walks the drift, keeping each setup whose release passed the condition, and the month's report shows its result against the drift's before the model's training cutoff and after it; while its version's history is still being read its verdict says it is not testable. From 21.1 the monthly test also walks the opposite condition beside it, a release whose results did not read better or whose outlook was lowered or withdrawn, registered nowhere and written nowhere, and prints both sides' trades and mean edge before and after the model's training cutoff, so the operator can rule on both conditions from one run's figures.

Business first

Business first reads the company before the chart. It buys a stock only where the business is profitable, growing faster and turning its profit into cash, and then only at the pullback's own buy point.

From 26 October 2026 its card, its tile, its buys and its account left Tonight, paper trading and Past picks, Fundamentals standing in its place, and its rule ran on out of sight, the Run page counting from 29 October 2026 how many picks its unseen trades held back each night. On 30 October 2026 the change that moved the S&P 400's and 600's rules took it out of the night: nothing is computed for it any more, every night it did run is kept, and its one open trade runs out under its own plan. Its business check was to become a version of each index's pullback, named "Pullback, business growing"; on 29 October 2026 the operator registered none, its own search having passed no setting on any index.

How to read it. Each layer is one test, the business ones read from what the company filed with the SEC, as first filed, each filing counting from the trading day after it was filed. The settings shown are the starting ones, the same on every index.

What to take from it. Because it buys only at the pullback's buy point, this family changes which stock is bought, not when.

GateWhat it asksWhy it is there
The pullback's baseDoes the pullback's rule at its base settings list the stock tonight?It is the one entry every index already replays and records, so the business decides which stock and the pullback when.
The index's floorsDo its price and daily dollar volume clear its index's floors?A stock too cheap or too thinly traded costs too much to buy and sell.
ProfitDid the four newest quarters' profits add up above nothing, and the newest on its own?The test S&P sets before adding a company to its indices.
Sales speeding upDid the newest quarter's sales beat the same quarter a year before, by more than the quarter before had?Stocks whose sales came in above expectations have tended to keep rising after the report (Jegadeesh and Livnat 2006).
Margins wideningIs the gross or the operating margin wider than a year before?More profitable companies have tended to earn more (Novy-Marx 2013).
Cash behind the profitDid the business bring in at least as much cash over its latest year as it reported as profit?Profit backed by cash has lasted longer than profit that is not (Sloan 1996), though trading on the difference has paid less since it was published (Green, Hand and Soliman 2011), so here it is a check on the profit and not a reason to buy.
The trend agreesIs the close above its 200-day average, and the 50-day above the 200-day?A good business in a falling stock can keep falling; the chart has to agree before anything is bought.

The trade. A stock passing all of that is bought at the pullback's buy point with the pullback's own stop, target and limit on holding time, at the next session's open, five a night in the pullback's order. An earnings surprise is no trigger here, since the drift after one has gone from large companies and more recently from smaller ones (Martineau 2022). Its card stands after the index's own families' cards, and on the S&P 500 it holds back a stock the index's own families listed that night. It lists on all three indices on its starting settings until you approve a change the monthly loop proposes for it on an index, from a grid of 27 settings of its three dials written into the code before any search runs; it registers no live rule and is never frozen, and a search run by hand is only a reading for you and changes nothing.

The short-term dip

The short-term dip buys a stock in an uptrend right after a sharp drop of a few days that no report caused, and sells the bounce within days. Every other family holds for weeks, so its rule takes years to gather enough finished trades to judge; the dip trades hundreds of times a year across the three indices, so its record can be judged in months. It buys weakness where the breakout and the drift buy strength.

How to read it. Each layer is one test, read from the stock's stored prices and the stored calendar of reports. The settings shown are the starting ones, the same on every index.

What to take from it. It buys a rising stock on its worst days, where the breakout buys it on its best.

GateWhat it asksWhy it is there
The index's floorsDo its price and daily dollar volume clear its index's floors?A stock too cheap or too thinly traded costs too much to buy and sell.
Enough historyDoes the store hold at least the 200 sessions its average reads, with no day the exchange traded missing and its prices not marked suspect?The night reads the stock's own stored year and no older history, so a recent listing short of 200 sessions is left out and its row says why. A missing day would make a two-day fall read as one day's, and a failed refetch after a split can leave a drop that never happened, so either leaves the stock out the same way.
An uptrendIs the close above its 200-session average?A dip is bought only in a stock that was rising; a stock falling for months can keep falling.
A sharp dropHas its 2-session relative strength index, a reading of how one-sided the last two days were, fallen under 10?Stocks that fell hard over a short spell have tended to bounce back over the next days and weeks (Jegadeesh 1990 and Lehmann 1990).
No report behind itDid no report fall on the three sessions to tonight, and is none due before the trade's time runs out?A fall with no news has tended to reverse, while a fall after news has tended to keep going (Chan 2003).
A typical moveDoes the night hold a typical daily move to place the stop by?The stop is set in typical moves, and every result is counted in multiples of the risk the stop sets.

The trade. A stock passing all of that is bought at the next session's open, its stop 4 typical moves under the close it was listed at and no target. Each day it is held its close is read: a close above the average of its last 5 closes, or a close under the stop, sells it at the next session's open, and where neither has come by the end of its 10 sessions it is sold at that session's close, a day known from the start. Five a night are listed, the most one-sided drop first. Its result is what it made in multiples of what it risked at the listing close, as every swing family's.

Its own market setting. The market check that closes every other swing family's list on a weak night does not close the dip's: falls like these have tended to bounce back hardest when the market is fearful and its volatility index high, even among the largest stocks (Nagel 2012), so the dip reads a setting of its own on every index, off while it is provisional. On such a night the top of Tonight says which lists the check closed and that the dip reads its own.

Last in the order, and giving way. Its card stands after business first's and before the sector heavyweights' on every index. It lists no stock another family listed that night, nor one any family holds an open trade on, on any index, and a stock it gave way on carries its label on the row of the family that listed it; no other family's list reads the dip's trades, so every older family's record and paper account stays as it was. Its starting settings follow the rule Connors and Alvarez published (Connors and Alvarez 2008), and it lists on them, marked provisional and counted toward nothing, until its test over the history on each index proposes the settings you freeze.

Tested on each index's own history. Its test reads every setting of its grid over each index's years since 2019, on the members the index held at the time, every trade bought at the next session's open, sold as the rule says and charged its cost: how deep the drop must be, which trend it needs, which exit sells it, how long it may hold, how far under the close its stop sits, and a market setting of its own; then the order of its list and three filters, each tried alone on the ten strongest settings and kept only where it helps in six of the eight years with two of the last three. The sector heavyweights

The sector heavyweights are a rotation, not a swing trade: once a month they buy the largest companies that lead their sectors and hold them for as long as they keep leading.

How to read it. Each layer is one test, read on the month's rebalance and not every night. A company is counted once however many share classes it lists, and on the S&P 400 and 600 a sector's return is the average of its members, since no fund holds an index's sector.

What to take from it. The book changes only once a month, and the market check never closes it.

Quality at a fair price

Quality at a fair price is a set of holdings, not a swing trade: once a month it holds the companies whose filings score strongest on the fundamentals scorecard and whose price is no higher than its own usual, and keeps each for as long as it still qualifies. On each index its holdings count toward nothing until its test over the past seven years is read and you fix its settings; on the S&P 500 they were fixed on 25 October 2026.

Close to a buy point. Each card keeps, folded behind a line counting them, the stocks one gate short of its rule, each with the gate it missed and the three numbers that card draws in place of sentences; none of them is a pick, and nothing counts them as one. A pullback's three are how far it came down in its own typical day's moves, how quiet its volume was on the way down and how strong it has been against the other members; a breakout's are the days since its high, its volume against its average and how far its daily range has narrowed; an earnings drift's are the size of the reaction, its volume and the days since the report. Each is the figure the night stored when it judged that stock: on the S&P 500 the rule's own answer, and on the S&P 400 and 600 the record kept of the stock's setup, an earnings drift's from the day of its report; where none was stored the cell says why.

Why a filter and not a score. Each gate is a fact you can check on the chart. A single blended score would hide which fact put a stock on the list. A learned score, once it passes its exams and you approve it, may order the stocks a rule's gates pass and leave its weakest off, but it never puts a stock on the list.

Why separate families. A pullback, a breakout, the drift after an earnings beat and a strong business at a buy point are different moments chosen for different reasons, so each keeps its own rule, its own five a night and its own record, and none is judged by another's. Stocks near their own recent high have tended to keep rising (George and Hwang 2004), and prices have tended to keep moving the way an earnings surprise moved them for weeks after the report (Bernard and Thomas 1989). The heavyweights are a different idea again, held for months rather than weeks: the industries that beat the others over six months have tended to keep beating them (Moskowitz and Grinblatt 1999), and stocks that swing more than the market have earned less for their risk (Frazzini and Pedersen 2014), a question the heavyweights' tests and records keep asking.

Why each index has its own rules. What works among the largest companies often fails among smaller ones, where fewer analysts follow a stock and trading costs more. So every family is tested and judged among its own index's stocks, and a change proven on one index is never carried to another.

7. How to read a pick's card

A stock is on tonight's list. What does its card tell me before I buy?

Each pick on Tonight has a card, opened beneath its row with Open plan and drawn on the stock's page after the short version. It gathers what a decision on that one trade needs into ten parts, in plain words, every figure in them worked out by the tool from stored prices and filings, never by a model. The picture below is a card with its figures blanked out as n.n and nn%, so it shows what each part says rather than any one night's numbers.

  1. One sentence first. Which setup picked the stock and on what night, that it is bought at the next trading day's open at about the plan's price, and what that setup buys, in a line.
  2. Why it qualified. A tick and a sentence for each step the setup checked and the stock passed, with the numbers the night stored, such as how far it beat its estimate and how heavy its volume was. On the S&P 400 and 600 a frozen rule's card names the rule that passed it.
  3. The plan. The setup's own, the one the trade follows: buy at the next day's open, sell if it closes below the stop, sell if it reaches the target, or for a breakout a stop that rises with the price and for the short-term dip its own exit, and otherwise sell after a set number of trading days, with roughly the date. Beneath it how much you could make for each dollar you risk, and once you have entered your account's size and how much you are willing to lose on one trade, how many shares that buys. A line says the trade is sold by these exits alone. Opened beneath a row of tomorrow's buys on the S&P 500, the card then draws the stock's entry and exit plan, the same price column and tables as its full page: zones to buy in below the price and zones to sell in above it, worked from the stock's own support and resistance levels, a separate plan with stops of its own that the setup's trade does not follow.
  4. Other checks. Whether the company makes money and earns enough to pay its interest, whether the market and the stock's sector are healthy, whether the stock is cheap to buy and sell, and how many of your open trades are in the same sector, each with a tick or a "!" and why in words. They advise and never remove a pick.
  5. What could go wrong before the trade ends. The next results date, or that none is on file, how far the stock moved on its past results days, when its next dividend comes off the price, and the Federal Reserve decisions and inflation reports due in the meantime, each with its date and a short why.
  6. How this setup has done. The setup replayed over years of past prices, after trading costs: how many trades it made, how many reached their target, the average gain or loss for every $100 risked, how long a trade usually lasted and its usual worst moment. It is the setup's record over every stock it bought, never a forecast for this one.
  7. Setups most like this one. Whether past setups most like tonight's, in the same kind of market and trend, did better, worse or about the same as the setup's usual, and what they were matched on.
  8. How it compares with the index's other stocks tonight. Seven headings, from momentum to the quality of its earnings, each a bar out of 100 with words saying what higher means, which is not always better: higher volatility means a jumpier stock.
  9. Your own trades of this setup. How the trades you took from it went, and beneath them how the setup's own picks on those same days went, so a gap between the two is your choices and your buying prices rather than the market of those weeks.
  10. I bought this. It records the trade, at the price you paid if you enter it; until the next day's opening price is stored the card assumes the plan's price. Beside it a link opens the stock's full page.

The checks in more detail. Five checks are on every pick's card: the gates it passed, whether the company made money and covers its interest, how the index's breadth and the stock's sector stand, whether its next report falls inside the time by which three in four of the rule's trades had ended, and how much trades in it a day with what a round trip costs against the trade's risk. On Tonight a sixth counts the trades you hold open in the stock's sector across the three indices, and warns at two, since stocks in one sector tend to move together. The card advises and removes no pick.

Setups like this one. On the breakout's and the drift's cards on every index, and the pullback's on the S&P 400 and 600, a part beneath the rule's record shows how setups like tonight's pick went under the same rule on the same index: the 250 nearest among the rule's finished setups on the measurements the learned score weighs most, never the pick's own stock, with their average result against the market that day and the range it would likely fall in. Beside it stands the rule's own average, and where the range takes that in, the card says the two cannot be told apart. The pick's place under the learned score is shown only once the score has passed its exams on that index; until then the card says the score is not yet validated there. On the S&P 400 and 600 a frozen rule's card shows this part only once the ledger's history has been built again under that rule, and says it is waiting for that build until then.

How often they won, and what they made. Opening that part, one sentence says what those same setups came to once the cost of buying and selling is taken off: how many of them reached their target before their stop, or for a setup with a rising stop and no target ended above their buy, and on average what they made or lost for each dollar risked, with the range that average falls in nine times in ten. For example: "Of the 250 past setups most like this one under this setup, 112 (45%) reached their target before their stop. On average they made $0.21 per $1 risked after costs." It is those setups' record, and the card says so: not this stock's chance, since a stock is not the average of the setups that resemble it. Fewer than 30 with a known result and the sentence is left out. The same average, worked for every stock a setup passes, is a way to order a night's picks, the ones whose past look-alikes did best first. On the S&P 500 the breakout carries one version ordered that way beside its others, judged by its own record from the night it was added. The drift there already holds nine versions, and a new version on the S&P 400 or 600 would change code their versions are fixed to, so the monthly test tries the order beside each of those drifts and breakouts whose walk it can order this way, without adding it, and prints what it came to; a version walked by its own search, as some frozen S&P 400 and 600 versions are, is not tried this way.

Ranking in place of pass-or-fail steps. A setup normally keeps a stock only if it passes every step. A ranking version keeps the event that defines the setup, a dip into a band, a new high, a report with its reaction, the dip's drop, and the market's and the price floors, loosens every other step, and then takes the five best of what is left that night in one order. Which order is read off how the past setups in each test year split into fifths: the learned score where its top fifth did better than its bottom fifth, otherwise what the setups most like each made, and no version at all where neither separates them. On 2026-10-30 the score separated the S&P 500's breakout and drift, the S&P 400's drift and the S&P 600's breakout, and nothing separated any pullback, the S&P 400's breakout or the S&P 600's drift. The S&P 400's drift and the S&P 600's breakout each carry a ranking version beside their others; the S&P 500's breakout and drift already hold nine versions, so the monthly test tries theirs without adding it.

The plan in your money. The card turns its plan into shares from three settings you save on Paper trading: your account's size, the share of it you risk on one trade, and the most one position may hold, a fifth of the account unless you change it. The shares are as many as the risk buys over the distance to the stop, rounded down, and never more than the cap buys. A heavyweight has no stop, so it is sized at an equal share of the account across the most holdings its book can hold, and the card says the whole position is at risk. The three settings stay in a file on your machine and in nothing the tool stores, logs or exports. With none saved, the card draws its plan in prices alone with a line linking the page where you set them.

What could hit the trade. The card lists what could jolt the price before the trade ends: how far the stock has moved on its past earnings reports against this plan's stop, the next ex-dividend date and what the dividend takes off the price, or, where the tool holds none of the company's dividends yet or none it can carry forward to a next date, that a later one cannot be ruled out, and any Federal Reserve decision or inflation report due inside the hold, days the whole market tends to move more.

I bought this, and your own record. The I bought this button records a trade you took, at the next session's open unless you enter your own price, and later its exit. A date you enter has to be a day the exchange traded, from the session after the pick's night on, and an exit cannot be dated before the fill or recorded on a trade the night has already ended. Each night the tool follows your trades under their rule's own selling rules and shows your record of each rule beside the rule's own, counting how many you won and lost and giving your average once twenty have ended. Beneath it the card counts the rule's own picks on the nights you took one, each bought at the next session's open as your trade is when you enter no price, so a gap between the two is your choice of picks and your fills rather than the market of those weeks. Past picks lists your trades under Your trades, and Paper trading gives them an account of their own. None of this changes which stocks are picked.

Why a card and not a verdict. Each part is a fact you can check, and the card removes no pick and reorders none. Whether to buy stays your decision, made with the facts that bear on it in one place.

8. What a report contains

When I open a stock, what do I get, and where does each part come from?

A stock's page is a report on one company, laid out in the order someone deciding whether to buy would ask their questions: what the price is now and what has happened, how the business is doing, what the price has done, where buyers or sellers have stepped in before and what the plan does there, what could go wrong, and what is coming up. Nothing on it is a forecast or a recommendation. Code works out every number from figures it has stored. A language model writes a few sections in plain words, and every figure it writes is checked against the stored figures and the documents it cites; a section that fails the check is left out and the page says so. Each part shows the day its figures are from.

  1. The top bar. The stock's ticker symbol and the company's name, the index it belongs to and its sector, the broad part of the economy it is in, and its price. While the market is open the price is the data provider's, about fifteen minutes behind the market, marked LIVE with its time; once the market closes it is marked CLOSED, showing the day's last price with its time until the night stores the closing price, and then that closing price.
  2. One sentence and four boxes. A sentence on what matters most about the stock now, with no figures in it, then four boxes: the company's latest profit for each share against what analysts expected, how fast analysts expect that profit to grow this year, or where no forecast is kept how much its sales over the last four quarters grew on the four before, its yearly dividend as a share of the price beside the government's ten-year bond, and how far the price is below its high of the past year.
  3. The contents. Every section below, numbered, in two columns. A section is marked NEW when it was written or filed since you last opened the page in this browser.
  4. How to read the page. What the page is for, the three things it will not do, which are predict the price, rank a company as an investment or say how much to buy, and what its words mean, folded shut until you open it.
  5. The short version. For a stock one of the rules picked, it opens on the pick, written by code: which rule, the buy, the stop, the target and the last day. For a stock a rule picked on an earlier night and still holds, it opens on that trade instead: the price it was bought at, the stop, the target, the last day its own rule gives it, where it stands and what it stands to make for each unit it risks at that price. Then the latest quarter, also written by code: sales and profit for each share against what analysts expected and a year before, and the operating margin against a year before. Then what worked and what did not, a few short paragraphs a language model writes last from the sentences of the other written sections that passed the check, using no figure the four boxes already show, each paragraph shown with four sentences at most, chosen by what they say rather than where they stand: the first on demand, then the first on costs, then the first naming one of the business lines, then the first on the main risk, kept in the paragraph's own order.
  6. The decision card. Shown only when one of the system's rules picked the stock that night: six quick checks on the trade, how that rule has done on every stock it bought before, the trade in your money and what could jolt the price before the trade ends. Where no date for the next results is on file, the card estimates one from the company's own results a year earlier and says whether it falls inside the trade; once the next day's opening price is stored, it states the price the trade would have filled at and what the trade stands to make for each unit it risks at that price. A stock a rule picked on an earlier night and still holds shows a short card for that trade in its place, with what the trade stood to make for each unit it risked at the price the plan was worked at beside the same at the price it filled at, saying so where the fill left it less to make than it risked; its plan and its chart show that trade's own stop and target. The earnings drift's record is shown beside the same rule replayed skipping any trade whose opening price left it less to make than it risked, a comparison that changes no pick.
  7. The latest quarter. The company's last three months line by line: its sales, its profit at each stage, its profit for each share and the cash the business brought in, each against the same three months a year before, and sales and profit for each share against what analysts expected before the results came out where EquityBrief kept it; analysts publish forecasts for those two lines alone, so the others say none published, and for the quarter that ends a company's year the data provider files no sales forecast apart from the year's, so that quarter is read against the year's forecast less the first three quarters, marked implied; a bank's or an insurer's sales are set against no forecast, since the analysts and the company's filing count a financial company's sales differently, and the line says so; with bars showing how sales and profit for each share grew over the last eight quarters. A quarter the company has announced but not yet filed is dated by its announcement and marked as from the results release until the filing arrives, and a report date already passed is said as reported, never as the next; where the next is not announced, the page gives the day it is likely to come, a year on from the company's report of the same quarter, marked estimated. Where the company has announced a newer quarter that neither the data provider nor a stored filing carries yet, the older quarter is shown beneath a line naming the newer one, the night the tool next asks the provider for it and the day its filing is expected, from how long the company took to file the same quarter a year before, so you know when the page should catch up.
  8. What management said. What the company's managers expect now against what they expected last time, and what they say is going well and badly, in their own words and checked word for word against their announcement. Where a newer results announcement has come out than the one these words were read from, its own opening line is quoted first.
  9. Segments. Where the sales come from: each of the company's business lines with its share of the whole and how it changed, set out this way only when the company's own figures for them add up to its total sales, and otherwise shown as its filing states them. Where the company has announced a newer quarter than the filing the tool holds, the business lines are read from the announcement's own tables when they add up to the sales it reported, shown first and marked as from the results release, and the written commentary on them is written from those figures; a commentary written from the older filing's figures says so above its text. Every amount in one table is shown in the same unit, so a $1,239.7M line never sits beside a $1.2B one.
  10. Margins. How much of each sale the company keeps as profit at three stages, drawn as three lines over the last three years.
  11. Fundamentals. Nine checks of the company's own filings with the SEC, as each was first filed, the same card the stock's chart page shows beneath its brief read: whether it made a profit and brought in cash over its last four quarters, whether its profit on its assets, its debt, its short-term cash cushion, its share count, its gross margin and its sales on its assets improved on a year before, and whether its cash beat its profit; each check ticked or crossed in a sentence with its figures, then strong, mixed or weak from the share it passed, and four lines beneath that score nothing but give context.
  12. Analysts. What the professional analysts who follow the company expect, how their forecasts have moved and how many rate it each way, every figure labelled as theirs and dated.
  13. Insiders. What the company's own directors, officers and largest owners bought and sold on the open market over the past year, as each reported it to the SEC: who, their role, the day they traded and the day they filed, how many shares, at what price and for how much. A purchase is green and a sale red, and a sale the filing says was set up in advance under a trading plan is marked planned, since a sale the seller timed says more than one a plan timed. Then the totals bought and sold over ninety days and over the year, and one line counting the shares given as pay, the options turned into shares and the shares held back for tax, which count in neither total because they are pay rather than a choice to buy or sell.
  14. Dividend safety. Only for a company that pays a dividend: whether its profit and its cash cover the payment, how many years in a row it has raised it, and how its yield compares with the government bond's.
  15. Valuation. What buyers pay for each unit of the company's profit, against its own past and against the S&P 500 companies in its industry, with a line saying which profit each figure divides by. Every figure here, the company's value, its yield and its multiples among them, is worked out from the last stored closing price, so the page shows one value for each; where the data provider's own figure was struck at a price far from that close, it is shown beside the worked one, marked stale with the price it was struck at. The industry is the data provider's classification, labelled so, unless you set one for the stock, and where the company's own business lines, as its filing or announcement names them, put most of its sales in a business the industry's name does not cover, the page says so; a description of the business is not read for this, since it names what a company sells alongside as well as what it is paid for.
  16. Earnings reactions. How far the price moved on each day the company reported its results, against how far it usually moves on such a day.
  17. The chart. The past year's prices with the price zones the system found shaded behind them, results days and dividend dates marked beneath, and the year's biggest moves numbered; for a stock a rule picked, the pick's stop and target drawn across it.
  18. The long view. The price at the end of every month over all the years the system holds, with the year's high and low and the plan's buying and selling zones drawn across it, so you can see whether the price has met those levels before.
  19. How it got here. The year's biggest price moves, each with what was behind it and where that explanation came from.
  20. Levels. The price zones above and below today's price where buyers or sellers have stepped in before, how far away each is in the stock's usual day's movement, and what the plan does at each. Where the past year holds no zone on one side, or none as far as a pick's target or stop, the prices the older history turned at are shown beneath, reaching as far as that target or stop and marked older, for reading alone: no rule or plan uses them. Where the history never turned at a price that far out, a line says so.
  21. The plan. Where the plan buys, in equal parts, and where it sells, with what each part risks and what it could make for each unit it risks; at the top it stops naming a price and follows the price up with a rising stop, and a part with nothing beneath it to stop at states no risk. A stock with no plan says why, and what would bring one. For a stock a rule picked, it opens on that pick's own way out, so it never reads as having none. The part on the next results opens on their date, given or estimated, and for a stock a rule picked or holds says whether it falls inside the trade or after its last day.
  22. How the rules read it. How each of the system's rules read the stock that night, opening on the checks of the rule that picked it where one did, the others folded shut beneath, and a check that failed said in plain words, such as which part of the trend test kept the stock out of an uptrend.
  23. The risks. What could go wrong, each with what would show it happening, then two lists, what would turn the stock up and what would make it worse, each opening on a line worked out from the plan at the price shown: a close above the nearest zone of sellers, or a close below the price at which the plan sells everything.
  24. The two cases. The case for buying and the case against, at most six points each, written by the language model, with no figures of their own.
  25. The industry cycle. Where prices in the company's industry sit in their usual rise and fall, and what is pushing them, read only from sources whose title or text, read in full, names the company or its industry; with fewer than two such sources it says so instead.
  26. Its group, by price. The other companies whose prices move most like this one's.
  27. News. What was written about the company over the past thirty days, each article sorted by kind and by whether it is good or bad news for the company, the articles not yet read in a group of their own. Only the company's own articles are shown and counted: one ticker can belong to another company on a foreign exchange, so an article must carry the company's American listing and name the company in its title or its opening lines, an article naming it only further down being about something else, and a line says how many were left out.
  28. Calendar. The next ninety days as a timeline and a table: the company's results, its next dividend, declared or estimated from how often it pays and drawn hollow when estimated, the Federal Reserve's rate decisions and the inflation reports, beside the dates the research found in the documents. Where no date for the next results is on file, the table gives the one estimated from the company's own results a year earlier, marked estimated, even when it falls after the ninety days, and for a stock a rule picked or holds it says whether the results fall inside the trade.
  29. Sources. Every document the written sections rely on whose title or text, read in full, names the company or its industry, the rest folded beneath, with a line saying so where fewer than two remain; any figure two sources state differently, and what the code worked out itself rather than read from a document. Each written section also lists its own sources by title beneath it, held to the same test with the rest folded apart, rather than marking its sentences with codes, and a code a writer used in place of a document's name is shown as the kind of document it is, such as the results release.
  30. Where the research stands. Whether the written sections are current, what writing them has cost, with each figure's scope: what the whole reports of the last seven nights cost against a pass that wrote only part of one, and the most any pass has cost with which stock, day and model setting it was, the button that asks for a new report, beneath any section the checks left out a button that has the local model write that one section again, at no cost and on any day, and a table naming which model did each job behind the page, the news labels, the reading of the results release and each written section, the model setting it ran under and whether that setting is paid or local.

The colours. Down the left edge of each section from How to read the page on runs a coloured rule saying what kind of section it is: green for the decision card and the plan, the parts about buying; amber, with the word Caution above it, for the risks, drawn dashed in the picture above; a muted plum for a part a language model wrote; and grey for the rest. A written part and a part taken from a company's filing each carry a column at the left saying the day it was written or filed, since either may be weeks old. Inside the plan the rows where it buys are marked green, the rows where it sells orange and the stop red. A figure that rose is written in green and one that fell in red, on this page and every other, each with its plus or minus sign, so the sign says it to a reader who cannot tell the two colours apart.

Where the figures come from. The price, the zones and the plan come from the stock's daily prices, which the tool stores every night. The company's results come from what it filed with the SEC, read from the SEC's structured facts or, where those do not yet hold a report, from the report's own data, and from the data provider's copy of the same quarters, a company matched to its filings by the filer number the SEC's own list of every listed ticker gives it, since the data provider's number can name the company that used the ticker before, and by the provider's only where the list does not name the ticker; where the two state a line of the latest quarter differently by more than rounding, the sources list both, the filing's first, and the page draws the provider's, since every quarter it reads comes from that one source. The analysts' figures are the data provider's, labelled as theirs and dated by when they were fetched, and the government's ten-year bond yield is read each night from the Treasury. The insiders' trades are read from the reports they file with the SEC, each night from the SEC's archive for the companies that filed and, for the years before, from the quarterly files the SEC publishes of every such report.

What the numbers say. Under the latest quarter, a plain sentence for each of four readings of the company's reported quarters, written by code from a fixed pattern, says whether sales and the operating margin are growing on a year before, how the company has done against analysts' estimates, whether its profits are backed by cash and where its price sits in its own range of earnings multiples. The heading gives the company's state, improving, steady or deteriorating, and says it is read from sales and operating margin alone, so a business can read improving beside profits that ran ahead of its cash.

Looking back. A stock's usual move on a results day is the middle of its moves over every report the stored and pulled history holds, a fall and a rise of the same size counted alike, and the long view's monthly prices reach back as far as the pulled history, adjusted the way the chart's prices are. How it got here takes each move's cause from the written research where there is some, otherwise from the stories published during the move as the news labeller read them, otherwise from the results and filings that fell inside it, and says so where it found none. The news is labelled by the model on your own machine, at no cost, and the page names which one wrote the labels; the labels a paid model wrote before it stand beside them, and the page draws the local one wherever there is one. The stocks on the S&P 500's list have their news labelled each night, and so does every stock a report is written for, on any of the three indices, so another S&P 400 or 600 stock's newer articles stand among those not yet read.

Which setup listed it. A stock one of the rules picked says, beneath the contents, which setup listed it, its place on that setup's card and any other setup it qualified under, and a stock a setup passed but held back for a trade still open says so.

Written words held to the figures. Every sentence written after the latest quarter was reported is read against that quarter's own figures before it is shown; one written before it is about the quarter before and is left as written. A sentence saying a margin narrowed where the figures show it wider, or that a line fell where it rose, is withheld, with what it said and what the figures show in its place. A sentence saying sales missed what analysts expected where the page's own estimate shows a beat is withheld too, and one sentence in its place names both: that its source counted a miss against its own estimate, and that the page's figures show a beat against the estimate the page draws. The causes written beside the year's biggest moves are read the same way. An abbreviation a company's announcement uses, such as DEPS for its diluted profit for each share, is spelled out the first time it appears. And every rule the page follows is checked over every stock's page, each passing, failing or not yet examined, a check not yet examined never counted as a pass.

Saving a report. A report can be saved as one file to hand to someone. It opens in the light palette with a button for the dark one, draws every figure without running anything, and shows the price the page showed when it was saved, marked as such.

Why this order. The page follows the questions a person deciding whether to buy tends to ask, in the order they ask them: what the price is and what has happened, how the business is doing, what the price has done, where the plan would buy and sell, and then what could go wrong and what is coming up. The business comes before the chart because a price means little without what the company earns beside it, and the plan comes before the risks so each risk is read against a plan already on the page.

Why so little is written by a model. A figure worked out by code can be checked against the stored data it came from, and the page draws it exactly. Written words cannot be checked that way, so a model writes only what figures cannot say, such as what management said in its own words, the case for and against and the risks, and last the short version and the headline from the sentences that passed. The next section says how each is checked.

9. Research, written once and kept

How is the written analysis produced, and how do we know it is not made up?

How to read it. Most opens stop at the top right: nothing has changed, so the stored research is shown and nothing is spent. The lower path runs only when something has actually changed, or when the night asked for the report.

What to take from it. Nothing expires on a timer. Research is rewritten only when a filing appears, an earnings date passes, the news spikes or you ask, and the night asks for six reports a night, taken in turn across the three indices' pages.

How claims are checked. Every number in the writing must match a number the system computed or read from a filing. Every claim must name a document that was fetched and stored. A sentence about the system's own figures, such as the closing price or an average, marks them with [N] instead, and a sentence that names only documents may quote only numbers those documents contain, so a company's results release cannot be cited for a share price it never mentions. Each written part may state only the figures its own part of the page draws and says the rest in words, so a figure is drawn in one place with its source rather than repeated in prose. The headline and the short version are written last, from the sentences the check accepted in the other parts, each sentence citing the ones it rests on, and the short version may not repeat a figure the tiles show or run past three paragraphs of five sentences each, since the parts beneath draw every claim in full. A section that fails is asked for again up to three times, and one still failing is left out rather than guessed.

Why so strict. The failure of automated research is a confident sentence that is wrong. Requiring a stored source for every claim means any sentence can be traced back to the document that supports it, months later.

Two models, and which writes today. Sorting and extracting from one document is small work a model on your own machine could do for free, and two short sections, what the company sells and the segment commentary, are set aside for it. Weighing the whole evidence into a view of the company needs a stronger, paid model. A small model given many documents tends to write figures the stored data does not hold, so the local lane is used only once a comparison on the same documents shows it writes only what the store holds. Until then every report is written whole by the paid model. The Research menu shows the local choice drawn but not offered until the two have been compared properly. Which local model it is, is a setting: each model has its own entry and one is flagged as the one used, the tool loads it and waits until it is ready before its first draft, and every section records the model that wrote it.

When a section is refused. A refused section is told each thing the check refused and asked for again, up to three times, and one still refused at its last try is left out, the page naming each rule it was refused by. Where the short version or the headline is left out, or no other part was accepted to write it from, the page puts one written by code from the night's own figures in its place, saying so. Every model is told who the reader is, someone deciding whether to hold a swing trade over the next weeks to months, and is given each document marked as a company filing, a news report or someone's opinion.

Seeing which sections keep failing. The run page lists each report section by section: passed first time, passed on its retry, or left out and why, with what each cost, and each section's rate over the last twenty reports. One setting names the model for each section, so a section that keeps failing can be given to another model. Before that, a trial can ask the other model for the same section beside a few reports, and a command writes the drafts side by side to a file without changing the report or showing them on any page. For now every report is written by DeepSeek, since a report written on Claude cost many times as much, and every job's setting names DeepSeek.

10. How the picks get better

How does the system learn which changes help, without fooling itself?

The improvement loop has a nightly half that records and a monthly half that proposes and tests. Once a month it reads what the nights stored, proposes changes to each family's rule on each index, tests each on years it never saw, and puts at most one change a family on each index to you. Nothing changes a rule until you approve it, and each index is judged and changed apart from the others. The four figures below follow one night, the test years, one month, and one month across the three indices.

The ledger, the loop's memory

Each night writes down every stock that came anywhere near a setup, not only the five a rule lists: every pullback, breakout, earnings drift, business-first and short-term dip setup that passes a much looser version of its family's gates, on each of the three indices, every company a monthly reshuffle finds with six or more of the nine fundamentals checks passing and every company whose newly filed report leaves it at six or more, each held as a book holding for up to a year against an equal-weighted hold of the whole index, and on the S&P 500 each of a sector's ten largest companies on the heavyweights' monthly rebalance, held as the rule would hold it. Each is stored with whether the live rule would have taken it, whether the night's list did, the plan it would have been bought on, its readings of the stock, its business and the market as they stood that evening, and later how the trade would have turned out against the same plan bought at the next session's open on every stock in the index. Nothing is read from a later day, so a row written tonight and a row rebuilt from the history for the same evening say the same thing. The proposals the loop puts to you are drawn from what these near-setups did, and never only from the stocks a rule happened to pick.

One reading asks whether a stock is cheap or dear against its own past: its last year's earnings as a share of what the whole company is worth at that evening's price, set against the same figure at the end of each of its last twelve quarters. Five readings describe the company rather than its chart: whether its sales grew against the same quarter a year before and whether that growth is speeding up, whether its gross and operating margins widened, and whether the cash its business brought in covers the profit it reported. They come from the figures companies file with the SEC, each as first filed, and only from filings made before the evening the setup is read on. To keep them fresh, after the close each night the tool reads the SEC's free daily list of filings, picks out the members that filed a quarterly or annual report, a correction to one or an earnings announcement, and asks for those companies' figures alone. A slow or unavailable SEC leaves the figures as they were and holds up nothing else. Nine more readings are the fundamentals card's: the share of its nine checks the company passed, its gross profit against its assets, its profit against its assets and how that moved on a year, and how its debt against its assets, its current assets against its current debts, its share count, its sales against its assets and its gross margin moved on a year, the debt's and the share count's read as falls so that a higher figure reads stronger. The monthly loop reads them from its first run after 7 November 2026.

Twenty-two more readings are measures researchers have published about a stock's recent prices and trading, added so the loop's engines have more places to look for why some setups win and others lose. None of them picks, filters or ranks a stock, and no rule changed when they were added; they are extra columns the engines may search, read from the stock's own days up to that evening:

  • return_week and return_month: how far the price moved over the last week and the last month.
  • largest_return: the biggest one-day gain of the last month, a lottery-like jump that researchers found tends to be followed by weaker returns (Bali, Cakici and Whitelaw, 2011).
  • volatility_month: how widely the daily moves swung over the last month.
  • residual_volatility: how much of the stock's daily movement over the last quarter was its own rather than its index's (Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang, 2006).
  • beta: how strongly the stock moved with its index over the last year (Frazzini and Pedersen, 2014).
  • skewness: whether the last quarter's daily moves leaned toward big up days or big down days.
  • close_place: where the price tended to close inside each day's range over the last two weeks, near the high or near the low.
  • up_volume_share: how much of the last month's trading came on days the stock rose.
  • price_volume_correlation: whether heavy trading went with rising or with falling prices over the last month.
  • illiquidity: how far the price moved for each dollar traded over the last month, high for a thinly traded stock (Amihud, 2002).
  • overnight_less_intraday: whether the last month's gains came between one day's close and the next day's open or during the trading day (Lou, Polk and Skouras, 2019).
  • sector_month and sector_quarter: how the stock did against the fund of its own sector over the last month and the last quarter.
  • low_ratio: how far the price stands above its lowest point of the year.
  • streak: how many days in a row the stock has closed up, counted as a plus, or down, counted as a minus.
  • volume_slope: whether volume on up days or on down days has been building over the last month.
  • alpha_one: Kakushadze's first published formula (2016), on which of the last five days the stock's close, or after a falling day its recent swing, was largest, placed against the other members.
  • alpha_two: his second, whether over the last six days the stock's jumps in volume came on days it rose from its open, each placed against the other members; a figure above nothing says they did not.
  • alpha_three: his third, whether over the last ten days the stock's opening price and its volume, each placed against the other members, rose and fell together; above nothing says they moved apart.
  • alpha_four: his fourth, where today's low, placed against the other members' lows, stands among its last nine days' places; the lower it stands, the higher the figure.
  • alpha_six: his sixth, whether the stock's opening price and its volume rose and fell together over the last ten days; above nothing says they moved apart.

Four more readings are about what the company's own directors, officers and largest owners did with their money, read from the reports each must file with the SEC within two business days of a trade, and only from reports filed in the ninety days before that evening, so nothing filed later can leak in. Only buying and selling on the open market counts: shares given as pay, options turned into shares and shares held back for tax are the mechanics of pay rather than a choice. Like the published measures, none picks, filters or ranks a stock, and they stand under the chart's analyst sentiment heading:

  • insider_net_buy: the dollars insiders spent buying the stock less the dollars they took selling it, against what the whole company is worth that evening; above nothing says they bought more than they sold. Researchers found insiders' buying says more about the price to come than their selling (Lakonishok and Lee, 2001).
  • insider_buyers: how many different insiders bought.
  • insider_officer_buy: one where an officer of the company bought, nought where none did.
  • insider_unplanned_sale: the dollars insiders took selling outside a trading plan they set up in advance, against what the company is worth. Researchers found the trades insiders time themselves, rather than routine ones, are the ones that carry information (Cohen, Malloy and Pomorski, 2012). The reports began saying which sales were planned in April 2023, so this reading is left empty for an evening whose ninety days reach before then.

Each of the four is left empty, never nought, for an evening the stored reports do not reach back ninety days.

Nine more readings came with the short-term dip, the ones its test reads that the list did not already hold, so the loop can look at them for every family:

  • rsi_two: the 2-session relative strength index, low after two sharp down days.
  • drop_fall: how far the price fell over the last three days, in the stock's typical days' moves.
  • drop_largest: the biggest single day's fall of those three, in typical days' moves.
  • drop_volume: how heavily the stock traded over those three days against its usual volume.
  • drop_against_sector: the stock's fall less that of the middle member of its sector on its index over the same three days.
  • large_breadth: on the S&P 400 and 600, the share of the S&P 500's members above their own 200-day average.
  • fund_over_long: the index's own fund against its 200-day average.
  • vix_over_median: the VIX against its middle level of the year before.
  • company_value: how large the whole company is at that evening's price, counted in powers of ten.

Five more came with phase 21, the ones you chose from the sources the tool checked first, each filled from where it can be read without paying for every stock every night:

  • Relative strength at a new high and insider cluster buying: read from the stored prices and the stored insider filings, so they reach back to 2019 like every other reading.
  • Upgrades less downgrades: each firm's dated rating changes as Yahoo lists them. Yahoo's list reaches back years, so asking each stock once fills its history; each morning, after the news is labelled, the tool asks Yahoo about the 80 stocks it asked longest ago, leaving 20 of the day's 100 asks for the chart pages you open, so every stock is asked about once in about nineteen days.
  • Estimate revision: how the analysts' forecast of this year's earnings a share moved over the last month. No source keeps what the forecast was on a past day, so each evening the night asks the provider for every stock's forecast, fifty stocks a request, and the reading starts from the first evening it was asked; the Ledger page says from when.
  • Short interest: the shares sold short over all the shares, from FINRA's twice-monthly reports, free, back to the end of 2017. Each report is read only from the day after FINRA published it, about a week and a half after the date it counts.

Once a month the loop also works out which readings went with winners, and the Ledger page shows it for each family on each index: how strongly each reading's value when a setup was found matched how that setup turned out, year by year from 2022, against the same figure with the results shuffled among each evening's setups so that a lucky match is not mistaken for a real one. A reading is said to go with winners or with losers only where it beats the shuffle. The words are for you to read; no reading is removed and no rule changes because of them.

How to read it. One night, along the top row and then the bottom one. Green boxes are arithmetic, blue boxes are stores the night writes, and the purple box reads from outside. The families draw the lists, and then the ledger appends the setups: every member and night a family's loose gates pass, kept or not, with its readings as they stood that night. Windows close as paths end, so a setup's result and its edge over the same plan on every member are written once its trade would have ended. The filings refresh reads the SEC's archive after the close for the members that filed and for no other, their business readings read again from the new filings, and the summary rewritten for the Ledger page.

What to take from it. The ledger is the loop's memory. It holds the setups the rules turned away as well as the ones they picked, so a change can be judged on everything it would have done.

Readings from results releases, by the local model

A model on your own machine, Gemma 4 in LM Studio, reads each company's quarterly results release with its names and dates removed and answers four closed questions: whether the results came in better than expected, in line, worse or not stated; whether the outlook was raised, kept, lowered or withdrawn, or none was given; whether management's own words sound confident, neutral or cautious; and whether demand is stronger, steady, weaker or not stated. Code turns each answer into a reading, an answer of not stated or none given counting as no reading, and the model is never asked for a number. It runs as a process of its own at 12:05 and 21:40 UTC each day, never inside the night, and spends nothing. Each run works under a pin: the model, its file checked by its fingerprint, the questions and the amount of text the model is loaded to take in are fixed and compared before the run starts, and the model that answers is checked on every answer, at a setting that gives the same answer to the same text, so the history read again answers as it did the first time; the runtime LM Studio serves the model with is written down and checked against nothing, since only the model decides the answer. Anything that differs refuses the run, and the Run page names the part. LM Studio's own saved setting for how the model writes its answers is checked as well before each run, since LM Studio applies it to every request, and a run refuses where it has changed since the history was read. Each run fetches the releases the companies filed since the one before, free from the SEC, and checks the pin: the morning run reads the SEC's list of each day's filings, and the evening run its live feed of filings back to the evening before, so a release filed after the close is kept before that night. A release is the press release attached to a company's results filing, kept once as text, compressed, with the exact moment the SEC accepted it, and the years before are fetched once by hand from each company's own filing history. Where a filing carries more than one attachment, the release is the one the filing's own index calls a press, earnings or news release, in its description or its file name, and the first attachment where it calls none so; a few companies file a long supplement of tables first and the press release after it, and the filings fetched before this rule are each checked once by hand, the supplement kept beside the press release that takes its place. Before a release is read, code takes out the company's names, now and before, the short names it gives itself, as BD for Becton, Dickinson and Company, and its initials, its tickers, the people it names and every date, year and quarter, putting a neutral word in brackets in each place, so the model cannot find the company or the period in its memory; a common word such as General or First goes only where it is the name and stays where it opens a sentence, while the company's own name goes wherever it stands, at the start of a sentence too and in its own spelling, as nVent, and the names of products stay. Each run reads the releases of the week before it, and the years before are read once by hand, around the clock over about three days with the graphics card held at 250 watts so it does not run hot. A release too long for the text the model can take in at once, about 160 of over 45,000, is read from its opening, which carries the headline, the quarter's figures and what management said, cut at the end of a line and marked where it was cut, and cut again a little shorter where the cut still left the model no room to write its answer. A new version of the reading asks again only what it would ask differently: each release whose question to the model would be word for word the one the version before asked, read whole with room left for its answer, keeps that answer however much text the model is loaded to take in, and the rest are read again, a release whose attachment changed, one the model could not take in at once, and one whose text a name learned since would change. LM Studio updates the runtime that serves the model by itself every so often, and an update asks for nothing: no new version and nothing read again, since the model's file keeps the fingerprint it had, and a file that changed is a new model, to which no answer carries. Every reading is kept with its four answers, or why it was refused, and each stock's page shows its newest release's answers under what management said, saying the local model read them with the names and dates removed. How well the model reads is measured before any rule reads a reading: its results answer against the surprise the data provider recorded for the same report, its outlook answer against the guidance figures where two releases state them, all four against 100 releases labelled by hand before the model reads them, each split before and after the model's training cutoff of January 2025; and three checks over 200 releases, whether the same text gets the same answer twice, whether the model can name the company from the text, and whether its answers change with the names left in. One rule reads the readings: the earnings drift's tone variant (section 6), which reads the results and outlook answers alone, through one reader of the stored readings and only under a reading version whose history has been read whole. Once a version's history has been read whole the four answers also join the readings every setup in the ledger carries, each from the newest release the night of the setup's session could read that the SEC accepted within the 95 days before it, none where that release's reading was refused, so the monthly run's engines can search them as they search every other reading, and the Ledger page shows what each answer came to, after the model's cutoff and before it.

The local model is also set beside paid work the tool already holds, to see whether it could take that work over for nothing. The first comparison relabels every news article the paid model labelled that is still kept, under the instruction that names each kind of story by a one-word key, the paid labels' kinds read as those keys, and counts how often the two agree on the kind of story and on which way it cuts, and how often the local model's reasons kept to words. Another asks it to write the report's sections over the same evidence the paid model was given for the one company the test data holds, through the same checker that refuses an unsupported figure, and sets each section's outcome beside the paid model's; its answers are recorded once, and from then the comparison runs with the tests. Another asks it again for each review a paid model made of its own drafts, over the same draft and the same night's facts, and counts how often the two reviews came to the same outcome and kept or changed the same sentences; a review whose night's facts the store has since trimmed is left out and named. A comparison is run by hand, writes its figures to a file and nothing to the store, and switches nothing: moving a job to the local model is your decision once you have read the figures, as moving the labeller was after its comparison.

Testing a change on years it never saw

A proposed change to a rule is only worth having if it would have helped in years nobody looked at when choosing it. The tester treats each year from 2022 as an exam: the search that proposes the change is run again using only the trades that had finished before that year began, whatever setting it picks is traded through that year, and the year's results are set against what the rule as it stands today did over the same year. So it is the way of choosing a change that is tested, not one lucky setting. A change passes only where four things hold together: the combined difference over all the exam years is too large to be luck, at a strict bar that stays the same every month; it was better in most of the complete years, the latest among them; it still helps once each side's five biggest results, good or bad, are set aside; and it rests on enough trades, or for the sector heavyweights, whose holdings last for months, enough months. Today's rules were themselves chosen with these same years in view, so the comparison leans in their favour, and the Loop page says so.

How to read it. Each row is one test year: Tests 2022, Tests 2023, Tests 2024, Tests 2025 and Tests the newest, the months since the last complete year. The green bar is what a proposal is built from, every setup and trade that ended before its year began. The orange bar is the year it is judged on, which it never saw, its trades against the rule's own over the same year. The dashed bar is drawn and tested, and it does not count toward the years a proposal must be better in.

What to take from it. A proposal is never judged on the data it was built from, so a change that only fits the past fails here.

Four engines that propose

Looking at how trades ended. The trade autopsy proposes a better way out of a trade. For each family on each index it reads what the rule's own finished trades did between buying and selling: how far the typical trade rose and fell before it ended, how many days its best close took to come, how many trades that hit their stop had first been one risk up, and how far the trades that reached their target first dipped. Those figures explain; they never decide. The ways out it can propose come from a fixed list written before any trade was read: moving the stop up to the buying price once a trade is a little ahead, trailing the stop under the highest close, a nearer or further target, selling a trade that has not moved after a set number of days, or selling half at the target and trailing the rest; for the sector heavyweights, also selling a holding that closes under its long average, or rebalancing every week. From phase 21 the short-term dip is tried under the same list, each way out sold at the next day's open as the dip itself sells, and the two business books, Quality at a fair price and Fundamentals, under their own: selling a holding that closes under its long average, for Quality also selling on the night a new filing drops its score below the floor, and for Fundamentals holding for a shorter or a longer time. A way out the family's own rule has no setting for, any of the list's for the dip and the long average's for the two books, is still tried and shown on the Loop page with why, and never put to you, since approving it would change nothing the nights do. Each is replayed exactly over every past trade and put through the exam years. A rule's settings already have a place for each one put to you, so an approved change is a stored setting or a rule registered again, and never new code.

Setting winners against losers. The second engine proposes an extra condition a stock must meet before a rule lists it. For every stock a rule could have listed in the past, taken or not, it asks whether the stocks that went on to beat the market looked different from those that did not on any of the ledger's readings. It tries each reading cut at nine points from low to high, either way round, and then a second reading inside the best first one. Tried that many ways, something always looks like it separates winners from losers by luck, so every idea is checked against the same search run again with the winners and losers reshuffled among the stocks of each day: a condition goes forward only if it beats what the reshuffled search finds nineteen times in twenty. Shuffling within a day keeps the market's own ups and downs out of it. Whatever passes then goes through the exam years, chosen each year using only the years before. From phase 21 it also reads the short-term dip's past setups, and for the two business books the stocks that qualified for each book, so a condition there narrows which qualifying stocks a book holds.

A learned score. The third engine proposes an order for a rule's list. It fits one weighted sum over the ledger's readings, each weighted by how much it told the stocks that went on to beat the market from those that did not, across the near-setups of all three indices that had finished before the year being tested. Every weight is pulled toward nothing unless many setups support it, the biggest runaway results are clipped so a handful cannot set the weights, and the one reading that peeks ahead in the history, the days to the next report, is left out. It proposes the rule's list ordered by the score, and the same with the lowest fifth or the lowest two fifths left off, each through the exam years, and from phase 21 it does the same for the short-term dip and the two business books over their own past setups. It is worked out by code from stored figures and gives the same weights when fitted again on the same setups, so it counts as a rule and not as a judgment.

A challenger of decision trees. Beside that sum the loop fits a hundred small decision trees by FastTree, a gradient boosting method from Microsoft's ML.NET library. Each tree asks a few yes-or-no questions of the readings and adds a little to the score at the end of each path, each fitted to what the trees before it still got wrong, so trees can find a reading that only matters together with another. The trees are stored as plain text the system walks itself, so a stored set scores the same way every time it is read. One of their proposals is put to you only where it passes and also earned more over the exam years than the weighted sum's proposal of the same kind, so the more complicated score has to earn its place against the simpler one.

Asking the local model for ideas. From phase 20 a fourth engine hands the same two tables, how the rule's trades ended and how the winners' readings differed from the losers', to the local model on your own machine, with no stock's name and no date in them, and asks for at most three conditions worth trying. It may only answer in labels: which reading, above or below, and which tenth of the stocks in that reading's order, one to nine. It never writes a number. Code turns each label into the level the second engine's search would cut at, the value at the top of that tenth among the stocks that year's own table holds, so an answer of the RSI, above, seven becomes "also requires an RSI at or above n.n" with n.n the top of that year's seventh tenth. The model is asked once a month, in a step before the exam, inside each exam year over that year's tables alone, and its answers are stored; the exam reads them as it reads any engine's proposals, scores each year's idea on that year, and counts its three proposals among the family's when it allows for how many ideas were tried. An answer not in that shape is asked once more and then refused, and in a month the model cannot be asked the exam runs with nothing from it.

How to read it. One month, along the top row and then the bottom one. The ledger and the rules' trades are what the nights stored. The engines propose: the autopsy an exit, winners against losers a condition, and the learned score, a weighted sum with a challenger of decision trees beside it, an order or a floor. The tester judges each proposal against the rule today on the years the proposal never saw. The Loop page draws what the tester stored and computes nothing, the orange box is you: you approve or decline the one proposal put to you for each family, and with your approval the change applied is written as a stored setting before the next night.

What to take from it. The system measures and proposes. Only you change a rule, one change a family a month at most.

Your word, one index at a time

Each month's report names, for each family on each index, the one proposal put to you, the strongest that passed, or that none passed. You approve or decline it on the Loop page, and a decline is kept with its reason; a declined proposal comes back only once a later month adds a full new block of evidence and it still passes. An approval is applied by the next night's own build, on the index it was approved on alone. On the S&P 400 and 600, where the family is still provisional, it becomes a stored setting of that family from the next night. On the S&P 500 a change to the breakout or the drift also becomes the family's registered live rule on the night it is applied, so its record starts that night, and the rule it replaced keeps running beside it as a variant for one block of 63 sessions before it retires, unless you approve a restore first. A change to the S&P 500's pullback, a way out, a condition or an order, is stored and read by the pullback's card from the night it is applied, and an approved move of one of its filter settings opens the filter's next settings version. A change to business first, one of its 27 settings or a way out, a condition or an order, becomes the family's stored setting on any index, the S&P 500's among them, from the next night, since that family registers no live rule anywhere. The approval setting ships asking for your word; the loop applies changes by itself only if you switch it to automatic.

How to read it. Each column is one index for one month: the large caps are the S&P 500, the mid caps the S&P 400 and the small caps the S&P 600, each with its own setups and its rules' own trades. Each index's proposals are tested on its years, against that index's own rules today. Its own Loop page draws them, and your word on one index is given there alone. An approved change is applied to it alone. On the S&P 400 and 600 it is a stored setting read from the next night. On the S&P 500 it is applied by the night: a breakout's or a drift's change is stored and registered as the family's live rule on the first night whose session a new registration can count from, the rule it replaced running beside it for 63 sessions; a change to the pullback's way out, condition or order is stored for the pullback's card from the next night; and a move of one of the pullback's filter settings opens the filter's next settings version on a night a registration can count from. A change to business first is a stored setting read from the next night on any index, the S&P 500's among them.

What to take from it. Nothing crosses from one column to another. A change proven on one index says nothing about another, and approving it touches no other index's rule.

A worked example: an autopsy finding and its change, on one index, with placeholders in place of figures. On the S&P 600 the autopsy reads every breakout trade the rule took over the history: the median trade's best close came n.n risks above its buy before its trailing stop ended it, and nn% of the trades the stop ended had first closed a full risk up. So it proposes the exit from its menu that keeps the most of that, a target at n.n risks. The tester runs that same choice inside each test year, on the trades that ended before the year began, and scores what it picks on the year itself: better in n of n counted years, still ahead with the five largest results set aside, and an adjusted p-value under the bar the month's run is held to. The month's report puts it to you as the breakout's one proposal on the S&P 600. You approve it on the Loop page, and before the next night the S&P 600's breakout reads that exit. The S&P 400's breakout, tested apart, stands as it was.

A worked example: a proposal passing on the S&P 600 and not carried to the S&P 500. Winners against losers finds that, on the S&P 600's drift, the setups bought while the VIX closed under n.n did better than the rest, beyond nn% of the within-night shuffles of its own search, so it proposes that condition, and it passes on the S&P 600's test years. The same search on the S&P 500's own history finds another cut or none, and is tested there on its own, since one index's result says nothing of another's. Approved on the S&P 600, the change is applied to the S&P 600's drift alone. The S&P 500's drift stands as it was, and changes only by a proposal of its own that passes on the S&P 500's test years and that you approve there.

A worked example: a proposal rejected on the years it never saw. The learned score is fitted on the setups of all three indices that ended before each test year, and proposed on the S&P 400's pullback as an order for its list, strongest first. On the years it learned on it looked strong. On the test years it was better in only n of n, and with the five largest results set aside its total fell under nothing. It did not pass: the Loop page draws it with each of its four parts and the one that failed, no press is offered on it, and the month's report puts nothing to you for the family. The rule stands as it was, and the score is fitted again next month.

The live alarm

Approving a change cannot catch a rule that stops working, so each night an alarm reads each live rule's trades by the month they ended in, or a heavyweights book's holdings by quarter, once the month has closed. It sets their average against the low end of what the rule's own trades over its test years would give by chance: the fifth percentile of ten thousand averages redrawn from them. A month of fewer than five trades neither counts nor breaks a run. Two counted months running below that floor flag the rule on Tonight and on the Loop page, and where an approval changed the rule, a restore of the setting before that change waits for your approval. The flag changes nothing by itself. Because the rule was chosen on its own test years, the floor reads high, so the alarm catches a rule that has broken rather than one drifting a little.

Every rule tested each month

After the proposals, the monthly run also tests every rule standing on each index the same way, on the same exam years: each variant against its live rule, and each live rule against the same plan bought on every member. A rule it cannot replay, such as one reading the analysts' estimates the tool keeps only from when it began storing them, is marked as not testable with the reading it lacks and the words naming what judges it instead: its record since it was registered, on Paper trading and the Run page. Since 21.1 the S&P 400's and 600's rules at their own levels, a heavyweights book at a setting off its six, and a pullback version's own plan are replayed through the same readings their sweeps and the record command use, so only a rule reading something the store holds no history of is left unreplayed. Those verdicts change nothing. They stand beside each rule's account on Paper trading, described in section 11.

What the loop can change

Every family the nights run can take a change you approve on the Loop page, on the index you approve it on: the pullback, the breakout, the drift, Fundamentals, the short-term dip, the sector heavyweights and Quality at a fair price, in the order their cards stand on Tonight. A change is an exit, an extra condition or a score order, and for the short-term dip and the two business books one of their own settings as well. A family still provisional on the S&P 400 or 600 takes it as a stored setting. A family whose rule is registered, the S&P 500's breakout and drift, every family frozen on the S&P 400 or 600, and the short-term dip and the two business books on every index, takes it as its rule registered again with the change made: the rule it replaces keeps running beside it for 63 trading days and is then retired, and where the family already holds nine rules it keeps running out of sight, counted in the family's allowance for luck and drawn on no page. A frozen family's own settings still move only by a new freeze, so a change to one is its exit, its conditions or its order, and a frozen sector heavyweights book can only take the sale under the long average. Business first left the nights in phase 21 and takes no change. On the S&P 500 the pullback's shape clock, below, also proposes moves to three of its filter settings: how strong a stock's recent return must be, how quiet the volume must be on the way down, and the minimum reward for the risk, each tested like any other proposal. The S&P 500's sector heavyweights books change their settings only by a freeze. How deep a pullback counts, whether the market gate applies at all, how far away the stop may sit and how healthy the market must be move only by your ruling. The rules for finding levels and building the report's plan do not change through this loop.

Monitoring that changes nothing

Beside the monthly loop, the S&P 500's pullback keeps two gauges, which watch and change nothing by themselves.

How to read it. The shape clock watches how many stocks each of the pullback's gates lets through on ordinary nights. Once sixty ordinary nights have run under one settings version, it may propose a move to a setting, and that move goes to the monthly tester like any other proposal. The edge clock follows the pullback's rule and its variants in the background, each differing in one setting, and shows at fixed checkpoints, after about two years and about three, which version's trades actually do better than they need to. What it shows is monitoring: the list changes only through a proposal tested on years it never saw and approved by you.

What to take from it. How many stocks the list passes can be tuned within months. Whether the picks make money takes years to answer with confidence, and the pages say so plainly, so an empty result reads as waiting, not broken.

Why so slow. Try enough settings and one will look good by luck. Studies of trading rules show that most apparent winners disappear once you account for how many were tried (Sullivan, Timmermann and White 1999; White 2000; Harvey, Liu and Zhu 2016). So every alternative is registered before it is measured, measured in the background where it cannot influence the list, and judged against a stricter bar when several are tested at once (Holm 1979).

Why years rather than a count of trades. Trades that overlap in time share the same market, so they are not independent pieces of evidence. The system counts evidence in blocks of time rather than in trades (Cameron and Miller 2015).

Why fixed checkpoints. Looking at results again and again and stopping when they look good finds false winners. Results are judged only at set points, the way medical trials handle the same problem (Armitage, McPherson and Rowe 1969; Lan and DeMets 1983; FDA 2019).

Why a limit on adjustments. Each time the live settings change, the evidence gathered for the old ones stops counting toward the new. So any change shows how much evidence it would throw away before you approve it.

Why the turned-away stocks count. Each gate is also judged by what it turned away. If the stocks a gate rejects would have done as well as the ones it lets through, that gate is not earning its place, and the Run page says so.

Where the starting settings came from

On the S&P 500 the settings each family started from were first chosen by hand, then replaced by a starting point the stored history supports. On the S&P 400 and 600 each family's starting settings stood until you froze the setting its own search on that index's history would run, each trade bought at the next session's open and judged after its trading cost, the strongest setting where none passed; and business first's stand on every index until you approve a change the loop proposes. A sweep replays a family's rule over the years of history the store holds under every combination of its settings, and for the pullback under many designs of each step, scoring every trade each would have taken as bought at the listing night's close, one open trade a stock. Each result is read as an edge over the same plan bought on every stock in the index that night, so a setting is not credited for a rising market. The sweep does not pick the single best result, because among that many tries one always looks good by luck. It looks for a plateau instead, a region of settings that all beat the odds year after year, and proposes the setting deepest inside it, the one that can be moved furthest in any direction and still hold. Beside it, it proposes a few variants, each changing one thing on a question the history left open. None of it takes effect by itself: a sweep writes a report, and its starting point and variants begin running only when you freeze them, after which only the trades that follow are judged.

Ideas a sweep's grid does not hold, such as switches that keep a list off a weak market, a stop touched during the day, only the night's best three, a trailing stop and shorter holds, are tried on a rule one at a time. One is kept only where it does better than the rule in most years, the latest among them, and stays better without its largest few results. The report says how many ideas were tried and how many luck alone would pass, and an idea that holds is registered as a variant rather than made the live rule, so its own record decides. Changes to a live rule come through the monthly loop above.

Why the system never applies a change itself. A rule that moved its own settings would tune itself toward whatever it saw last. Every change also restarts the evidence for the rule it replaces, so it should be a deliberate choice with a written reason.

11. Paper trading

How would each rule's picks have done in money, and which rule is doing best?

Paper trading turns each rule's trades into an account of the size you choose, so the rules can be compared side by side in money. Each index has its own accounts: one for each family's live or provisional rule, drawn from the page's own list, one for each registered variant's own list or book, and one called You for the trades you recorded from the cards. The page reads from the top: a line naming your account, a tile for each family's rule beside the index's fund with the chart beneath, what the rules bought, what the variants did differently, each rule's scorecard, and your own trades.

How to read it. Each rule answers two questions, and its scorecard draws them as two halves. On the left, how the rule did over the test years in the monthly per-rule test, which is ready as soon as one month has run. On the right, how its own trades have done since the rule's current version went live, which says little until it holds as many trades as the test judges on.

What to take from it. A few good weeks are not evidence. A rule is read as winning only when both halves agree, and the page never adds the two together.

How an account is walked. Each account is worked out from your three settings when the page is read, a session at a time: the trades ending on a session are sold first, then that session's buys are made in their listed order, each sized off the account's value as a pick's card sizes its plan. A buy the free cash cannot cover is skipped and counted. Each trade pays its round trip at the published cost for its company's size and price, half at its buy and half at its sale. An open position is marked at its stock's closes, and a heavyweights book holds an equal share across the most holdings it can hold. No store, log or export holds a figure of an account.

Your settings and the example account. The line at the top of the page names your account's size, the share of it risked on a trade and the most one stock may hold, and says each rule trades its own copy of that account. Change the account opens the form that saves them to a file on your machine and nowhere else. Until you save them, every account is walked on an example the page names at its top, a hundred thousand dollars with one per cent risked a trade and at most a fifth in one stock, so the tiles and the chart show on your first visit. The example is never written anywhere.

The tiles and the chart. A tile for each family's live or provisional rule gives its account's return since its record began, how many of its trades are open and how many closed, and the day it counts from, with the rule's words under your pointer. The scorecard. Choose a rule and its scorecard draws its two answers as two halves, never added together. On history, under "Did it beat the easy alternative?", a box for each test year says whether the rule beat what it was compared with that year and by how much, in risks a trade or for a heavyweights book in points a month. Under "Could that just be luck?", a scale in three zones, easily luck, maybe luck and real, marks how often a rule picking at random would do as well, said in words such as about three times in a hundred, beside the line a rule must pass to count as real: rarer than one in two hundred and forty, the bar the monthly test holds a rule to. Its variants fold out beneath, each with its verdict, the years it beat the live rule and its own count since it went live. The exact figures behind the words, such as the adjusted p and the version a rule counts, stand under your pointer.

Your own trades. At the foot your own trades stand as an account apart from the system's: how many and how many have ended, their return, their worst drop and their return less the index's fund, the day they start and a link to their trade log. No test reads them.

Where an account starts. Each account is evidence about one rule that has not changed, so it counts only the trades of the rule's current version, from the same day the Run page counts that rule's record from. When a rule is registered again, after a change to its code or settings, its account starts again from then, by itself, and no one picks the date. The trades the earlier versions listed are still kept, folded beneath what the system bought and in the account's trade log, each named by the version that listed it, but they are in none of the account's figures. Each tile says the day its account starts, and a variant's row says it under your pointer with the version it counts. A provisional rule has never been registered, so its account, like your own, counts every trade from its first.

What it does not do. A variant's trades form its account's trade log on Past picks and never appear on Tonight, and a variant is adopted only through the monthly test and your approval on the Loop page. Every entry is bought at the open of the session after the night that listed it, as a trade you place after the signal would be, with its stop and target where that night's close set them.

Why two answers, kept apart. The verdict on history is ready at once but was read over years the rules were partly chosen on. The record since a rule went live is untouched by that choice but takes months to mean anything. Drawing both as two halves, never added together, lets neither stand in for the other.

Why an account restarts with its rule. A record that mixed trades from before and after a change would describe no rule that exists. Starting each account from its rule's current version, by a fixed rule rather than a date chosen after seeing the results, keeps it evidence about one unchanged rule.

Every page says it is a read-only copy and that it is paper trading only, nothing in it advice. It is never sent until you have read the report the first run writes, which names everything it found in the files.
